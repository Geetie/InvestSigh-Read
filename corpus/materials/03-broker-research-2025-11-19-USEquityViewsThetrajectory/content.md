The trajectory of AI capex and the next beneficiaries of corporate AI adoption
The 3Q earnings season has catalyzed another move higher in AI capex estimates alongside renewed investor concerns surrounding the risks associated with the AI investment boom. Consensus 2026 capex estimates for the AI hyperscalers (AMZN, GOOGL, META, MSFT, and ORCL) have risen from \$467 billion (+20% year/year growth) at the start of the earnings season to \$533 billion today (+34% year/year).
The “AI trade” has remained concentrated within the infrastructure complex, but return dispersion across that complex has increased. Much of the dispersion has been driven by the degree of investor confidence that AI investments are generating revenue benefits. The magnitude of leverage used to fund those investments has also contributed to the return dispersion.

The outlook for AI capex and the US equity market AI trade
A recent wave of corporate investment announcements and earnings results have reignited investor debate surrounding the magnitude of and potential returns to AI capex spending, driving differentiation within the AI infrastructure complex.
Spending estimates have taken another leg higher during the last several weeks. Since the start of the 3Q 2025 earnings season, consensus 2026 capex estimates for the AI hyperscalers (AMZN, GOOGL, META, MSFT, and ORCL) have risen from \$467 billion, representing 20% year/year growth, to \$533 billion today, representing 34% year/year growth. However, the dispersion of share price reactions to these increased spending estimates has demonstrated heightened investor scrutiny on capex spending. Investors have rotated away from AI infrastructure companies with elevated leverage, such as ORCL and CRWV, and where operating earnings growth is under pressure, such as META. In contrast, companies demonstrating a clear link between capex and revenues, such as the cloud platforms within GOOGL and AMZN, have been rewarded. The average stock price correlation across the large public AI hyperscalers has declined from 80% in June to just 20% today.
In the months prior to the latest announcements, the extraordinary continued strength of AI investment spending helped drive sharp outperformance of AI stocks. The GS AI basket (GSTMTAIP) has returned 40% YTD, outperforming the equal-weight S&P 500 by 34 pp. This has occurred alongside a simultaneous 80% increase in consensus estimates for forward 4-quarter hyperscaler capex spending, which have risen from \$280 billion at the start of 2025 to \$500 billion today.

Within the universe of AI-exposed stocks, equity gains have remained concentrated in the infrastructure complex. AI infrastructure, which we have categorized as “Phase 2” of the AI trade, includes semiconductors, hyperscalers and other data center operators, technology hardware, and power companies, among others. While most investors believe the AI adoption will eventually boost economic productivity growth and benefit an expanding universe of companies, the practical focus of most investors has centered primarily on the near-term earnings beneficiaries of the current boom in AI investment spending.
The share prices of Phase 2 infrastructure stocks have outpaced the trajectory of near-term earnings growth, indicating expectations of strong earnings growth for years to come. The average stock in our Phase 2 basket has returned 35% YTD compared with a 9% increase in its consensus two-year forward EPS estimate. The timing of an eventual slowdown in capex growth poses a risk to these companies’ valuations. While the large public hyperscalers’ capex spending has surged in the last few years, it remains below the precedent indicated by previous technology investment cycles. Among large public companies, operating cash flow continue to fund the vast majority of capex investment spending. During the past 4 quarters, aggregate capex among the large public hyperscalers has equated to 64% of cash flows from operations (“CFO”). Although this is far above their 40% average ratio during the past decade, in the late 1990s, S&P 500 telecom company capex spending equated to roughly 100% of CFO even before cash flows contracted in late 2000. Likewise, across the US economy, AI capex has recently equated to 0.8% of GDP compared with peak impulses reaching 1.5% of GDP or greater during other technology investment cycles during the past 150 years.

The strong balance sheets of the largest hyperscalers - and their recent willingness to employ those balance sheets - support the outlook for continued capex spending growth. The current distribution of valuations across the equity market suggests the hyperscalers could likely add substantial debt before experiencing materially negative consequences for their multiples on the basis of leverage alone. While the equity market typically assigns a valuation premium to companies with strong balance sheets, there is little differentiation between companies at the upper end of the IG spectrum. For example, the median Russell 3000 stock rated A+ trades at a 14x EV/EBITDA multiple, roughly the same multiple as the median stock rated AA or higher. This is despite roughly a turn difference of net leverage between the two groups of stocks (0.4x for AA or better vs. 1.2x for A+). AMZN, GOOGL, META, and MSFT are all rated AA or better, trade at a median of 12x EV/EBITDA, and carry a median net debt/EBITDA ratio of 0.1x.

If these companies decide to add leverage to their balance sheets to fund AI investment spending, debt market capacity, rather than balance sheet capacity, is likely to be the limiting factor. Excluding ORCL, the large public hyperscalers could theoretically increase their debt by \$700 billion by lifting their net debt/EBITDA ratios to 1x, keeping their leverage ratios below that of the median A+ rated firm. However, that magnitude of issuance would equate to 38% of the total volume of gross USD IG issuance during the past 12 months.
Supply bottlenecks could also act as a constraint on hyperscaler capex. During the 3Q earnings season, many of the hyperscalers continued to highlight that the supply of AI infrastructure could not keep pace with AI demand growth. Some companies noted that the supply constraint has shifted from chips to power. Our commodities team recently discussed how power bottlenecks are likely to at least temporarily slow AI capacity in coming years, and some companies have noted a substantial backlog in electric equipment such as gas turbines. However, the bottlenecks may incentivize additional investment to increase supply as well as lift prices and therefore the total nominal value of capex spending.
Investor willingness to endorse substantial capex upgrades for well-capitalized large AI hyperscalers will likely depend on the path to AI monetization and underlying cash flow strength. During 3Q earnings season, investors rewarded the AI hyperscalers that correlated increased capex spending with revenues. Negative share price reactions to capex surprises could force managements to reconsider the magnitude of capex growth going forward. Other risks to AI capex include a macroeconomic shock that erodes cash flows or tightens financial conditions or a surprise in AI technological development. With regard to hyperscaler capex specifically, the growing importance of neoclouds and other competitors may lead to a more fragmented capex build-out relative to the last few years.

The feedback loops created by revenue-generating business relationships as well as the equity stakes some of the largest US public companies have taken in smaller AI firms increase the risk that stress in one part of the AI ecosystem affects investors across the AI complex. For example, during 3Q 2025, S&P 500 companies generated \$40 billion in “other income.” This accounted for a collective 7% of S&P 500 profits during the quarter, including a \$10 billion unrealized private equity gain for GOOGL, a \$7 billion gain for AMZN, and a \$4 billion write-down for MSFT.
While the degree of public company leverage remains small, a continued shift toward debt financing would increase the macro risks associated with the AI build-out. Our Global Markets team has noted that, from a macro perspective, the current AI boom more closely resembles the environment of 1997 or 1998 than that of 1999 or 2000, with the different degrees of debt financing and corporate leverage a key distinguishing feature.
