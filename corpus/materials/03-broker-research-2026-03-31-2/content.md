With the jury decision in California finding Meta and Google guilty of designing addictive
products with mental health implications, doomsday scenarios and the social media morality
debate have returned. Is this Big Tech’s Big Tobacco moment? Every new media has endured
a moral crisis, most resolved with self-regulation and minimal impairment. While simple
extrapolations of the verdict look scary and expensive, we urge patience over extrapolating
legal outcomes.
A guilty verdict: In K.G.M. v. Meta et al., a jury found that the defendants deliberately
designed addictive platforms that harmed mental health, awarding $6M ($3M
compensatory, $3M punitive). Meta was assigned 70% of liability and You T ube 30%;
Snapchat and TikT ok settled separately for an undisclosed amount. Both Meta and Google
plan to appeal.
New media has been the consistent target for morality accountability. There’s a
long history of moral panic over new mediums. Congress linked comic books to juvenile
delinquency, prompting the Comics Code Authority. The PMRC hearings targeted music
lyrics, producing the “Parental Advisory” label. Congressional threats over Mortal Kombat’s
violence led to the creation of the ESRB game ratings system, while lawsuits linking Doom
to the Columbine shootings ultimately failed.
Why this case is similar, but different, to previous generations. After the 2011
Supreme Court ruling that violent media content can’t create liability, and the online
protections offered by Section 230, there’s little legal wiggle-room to blame digital media
platforms’ content for negative societal outcomes. Instead, the recent trial successfully
argued that the design of these products - algorithmic recommendations, infinite scroll,
and autoplay - knowingly caused addictive behavior leading to negative mental health
outcomes… hence the Big T obacco comparison.
Is Big Tobacco really a fair comp? No, though its evidence of known harms,
addictiveness, and large damages across a broad range plaintiffs makes for a compelling
narrative. The Tobacco MSA resulted in $200B+ in financial penalties for tobacco
companies, which extrapolated to the ~2-10K similar cases and then the billions of global
social media users adds up quickly! But crucially, mental-health causation is far more
individualized than tobacco’s physical harms thus harder to prove and social media’s role
in the process remains contested. Social media also offers offsetting benefits (community
support, self-expression, etc.), and product design could well be viewed as editorial and
shielded by Section 230 — raising the prospect of Supreme Court review.
While regulatory process unfolds, the second-order risk may be greater. We expect
the case to be tied up in the Appeals for the foreseeable future. The best defense likely
rests in (1) arguing that Section 230 also covers app design choices as editorial in-nature,
similar to deciding which articles appear where in a newspaper layout, and (2) pushing back
on the ‘significant’ contribution to mental health vs. everything else. More importantly,
we expect this verdict to galvanize stricter nationalist policies for social media use
affecting users and engagement. Stricter age limits, algorithm warning labels, and bans
on infinite scroll are already being explored worldwide.

DETAILS
THE VERDICT DETAILS
Jurors find Meta and Google guilty. In K.G.M. vs. Meta et al., the plaintiff sued Meta, Google’s You T ube, Snapchat, and TikT ok
for designing addictive products available to her at a young age that caused numerous mental health issues. A jury found the
defendants guilty of playing a substantial role in the harms experienced by the plaintiff. Meta and Google have both announced
plans to appeal the verdict. Snapchat and TikT ok were also named dependents, but both settled for an undisclosed amount prior
to the verdict.
• The first of many? The case was tried in a California state court and presided over by a jury. At the core, more than 2,400
lawsuits against Meta and other social media companies that make similar claims have been centralized in the California
federal court. Alongside the federal actions, more than 40 state attorneys general have filed their own suits, signaling
expanding government involvement. Beyond these structured groups, the overall universe is far larger—estimates point to
10,000+ individual claims and hundreds of school district cases alleging similar youth mental health harms caused by social
media platforms.
• What was the charge? The plaintiff alleged that she started using You T ube at 6 years-old, and Instagram at 9 years-old with
usage peaking at ~16 hours/day across the platforms. The plaintiff argued that these platforms failed to warn her of known
risks associated with platform use, and significantly contributed to developing anxiety, depression, body issues, and suicidal
thoughts.
• Defective product not harmful content. Given Section 230 offers platforms protection from user-generated content,
the case specifically focused on the product itself - the content recommendation algorithms, autoplay, infinite scroll, reward
loops etc. Plaintiffs argued that these delivery mechanisms were the defect similar to any other physical product. Documents
revealed during the trial suggested that platform management knew of the negative mental health outcomes associated with
product usage specifically for teenage girls.
• A guilty outcome. T otal damages awarded was $6M ($3M compensatory, $3M punitive) with Meta assigned 70% of the
financial damage and Google’s You T ube got the other 30%. T o note, the judge has not yet signed off on the verdict and may
override punitive damages.
• A lengthy appeals process ahead. Both Meta and Google have already announced their intentions to appeal the verdict
point at the complexity of assigning responsibility on mental health outcomes and platform definitions. Which points to two
clear legal defenses likely to be tested on appeal:
• Protection under Section 230 for editorial decisions. Digital services are different to physical product, and product
design decisions cannot therefore be defective as they are editorial decisions similar to sorted articles in a newspaper. If
the algorithm is considered editorial, then it likely falls under Section 230 protections.
• Complexity of mental health pros and cons. The defense had argued that the plaintiff had pre-existing mental healthy
issues, and indeed defining significant contributors to mental outcomes is far more complex than physical medical effects
from tobacco, for example. And to further complicate matters, there are certainly positive mental outcomes from these
platforms around connectivity, support, and community.
A VERY BRIEF HISTORY OF NEW MEDIA’S BATTLE WITH THE MORALITY POLICE
There is a remarkably consistent track record of unregulated new media causing moral panic: Comic books/cartoons
associated with juvenile delinquency; rock music lyrics promotion of sexual violence and teen pregnancy; video games
simulating shootings and glorifying violence, TV rotting the brain, etc. For now, the resolution appears grounded in selfregulation and content protections.
• New media a consistent target for morality accountability. There’s a long history of moral panic over new mediums.
• In 1954 Congress held hearings with comic books’ and cartoons blamed for juvenile delinquency and corrupting youth
resulting in self-regulation with the Comics Code Authority Act.

IS BIG TOBACCO A FAIR BEAR CASE COMP?
Similarities with Big Tobacco. We understand why evidence highlighting internal knowledge of negative health outcomes
and addictive properties alongside potentially expensive damages across a broad range plaintiffs creates a useful narrative. The
Tobacco MSA resulted in $200B+ in financial penalties for tobacco companies, which extrapolated to the ~2K similar cases
and then the billions of global social media users adds up quickly!
• The T obacco MSA emerged from a wave of early-1990s lawsuits in which U.S. states sought reimbursement for rising
healthcare costs tied to smoking-related diseases. Unlike earlier individual cases, these suits argued that tobacco companies
had known about the addictive properties and associated health risks of the product, and failed to inform the public of these
dangers. Here is where the parallels are strongest to social media.
• The settlement obligated tobacco companies to pay more than $200 billion to states over time, imposed major limits on
advertising and youth-directed marketing, and required the publication of internal industry documents. It also funded antismoking and public-health campaigns.
• The MSA had sweeping effects: it helped drive down smoking rates—especially among young people—redefined how
tobacco could be marketed, and created a legal model for governments pursuing large-scale settlements over public harms.
The parallel risk for the digital platforms could be a similar impact on youth engagement which may further put
pressure on future adoption and usage.

coverage as editorial in nature, which could see this case appealed to the Supreme Court.

SIZING THE IMPACT
1ST ORDER IMPACT: DIRECT LEGAL DAMAGES MAY BE MORE LIMITED THAN SIMPLE EXTRAPOLATION
At the core, more than 2,400 lawsuits against Meta and other social media companies that make similar claims have been
centralized in the California federal court. Alongside the federal actions, more than 40 state attorneys general have filed their
own suits, signaling expanding government involvement. Beyond these structured groups, the overall universe is far larger
—estimates point to 10,000+ individual claims and hundreds of school-district cases alleging similar youth-mental-health
harms caused by social media platforms. Following the trial outcome, we expect the number of claims to continue growing and
potentially culminate in a landmark settlement similar to the tobacco case.
• The settlement number will likely be a high bar watermark for similar cases. Of the $6M awarded, $3M was punitive
damages—an amount that is especially vulnerable to overturn from the presiding judge or on appeal. It’s also likely that
the K.G.M. case was chosen by the plaintiff’s attorneys because it had particularly strong supporting evidence, making it
a strategic first test case, and other cases may have weaker evidence or less direct relevance, potentially leading to lower
settlement values and win rates.
• Verdict precedence may carry less weight than expected. Unlike T obacco, mental health outcomes are far more debated
in medical circles, which makes cases far more individual and unique than a class-action would in the case of a product
causing measurable physical health issues. Each case would need to be tried on their own merits, their own usage of each
product, their own pre-existing conditions, their own environment, their our outcomes, etc.

2ND ORDER IMPACT: THE REGULATORY CHANGE DAMAGE MAY BE GREATER
• While we expect the case to be tied up in the Appeals process for the foreseeable future, more importantly, we expect
this verdict to galvanize stricter nationalist policies for social media use affecting users and engagement. Tighter
rules on age limits, specific warning labels on algorithm design and banning of infinite scroll, are areas already underway in
numerous countries around the world. Governments globally are looking at social media regulation under some key lenses:
1. Age gating is one of the most active spaces. Australia already implemented under-16 ban. Countries such as France,
Denmark, Spain, Brazil are also moving toward coordinated age-limit legislation
2. Product design, which is where this Meta/You T ube verdict lands, and pushes the spotlight to how these platforms are
engineered to cause “addiction” (features such as autoplay, infinite scroll, notification triggers)
3. Algorithm transparency related to forcing social media platforms to disclose exactly how their recommendation engines
work and giving users opt-out rights
4. Content moderation or shifting the liability onto platforms to proactively detect and remove illegal/harmful content
5. Data privacy / profiling under which there is greater push towards banning behavioral targeting re: minors and limiting data
collection to what is strictly necessary for service delivery
• The Australia experience shows us these things are not so simple. Under-16s in Australia have been banned from using
major social media services including TikT ok, X, Facebook, Instagram, You T ube, Snapchat and Threads. A few months into the
ban, there has been a mixed reaction from teens and experts. T eens are finding ways to circumvent the law (using VPNs and
creative ideas) and turning to alternative little-known social media platforms that are likely to have fewer and less stringent
controls. Social media has served as a critical mental health access point and support network, and 73% of young people
across Australia accessing mental health support did so through social media. The Australian Human Rights Commission has
highlighted that a social media ban could curb right to freedom and cause isolation, limiting ability to access information and
support which is particularly important for young people from marginalized, vulnerable or remote communities.
• A potential age ban could affect Snapchat the most and Meta least. We estimate that ~10-20% of users on Snapchat
are teens, followed by TikT ok and You T ube, with Instagram and Facebook having the least percentage. The revenue
percentage from teens is likely lower due to the limited monetization potential (lower purchasing power and strict targeting
rules) for teens account. Meta’s revenue from teen accounts is relatively small at ~1% according to recent testimony from
Zuckerberg.
• The engagement impact beyond teens may be greater, yet harder to quantify. The relationship between engagement
and revenue is complicated: On the one hand, lower engagement can reduce impressions/ad inventories, also causing
advertisers to pull back if they believe platforms are losing attention, and limit algorithmic optimization room given fewer
impression choices, creating secondary pricing pressure. On the other hand, fewer impressions can push prices higher due
to the supply–demand dynamics, especially if advertisers lack strong alternatives. Also, platforms may have not fully sold
their ad inventory currently, meaning impression declines may be smaller than engagement declines. Prioritizing highervalue impressions can also help offset losses. Specifically for Meta, it’s also exploring other new ad surfaces like Threads and
WhatsApp, which can potentially offset some of the headwind.

OTHER ONGOING LEGAL EXPOSURES FOR META

NEW MEXICO VS. META AND OTHER TEEN PROTECTION CASES
On March 24, 2026, a Santa Fe jury ordered Meta to pay $375 million for misleading users about platform safety and enabling
child sexual exploitation. The case stemmed from an undercover investigation in which authorities created a fake profile for a
13-year-old girl that was quickly flooded with sexual content and predatory messages. Over the trial, prosecutors presented
internal Meta documents and whistleblower testimony showing executives knew the platforms endangered children but
prioritized profits over safety. The second phase begins May 4, when a judge will decide whether Meta created a public nuisance
and must pay further damages and implement mandatory safety reforms. Meta has said it will appeal. This marks the first time a
U.S. state has successfully taken a major tech company to trial over child safety, and is expected to spur similar actions from
attorneys general nationwide along with the K.G.M. case.

FTC VS. META (ANTITRUST)
The FTC sued Meta in December 2020, alleging the company illegally maintained a monopoly in personal social networking
by acquiring emerging competitors Instagram in 2012 and WhatsApp in 2014. FTC demanded to force the divestiture of
both platforms, which would effectively dismantle the company’s core structure. After years of procedural delays, the case
proceeded to trial in April 2025. In November 2025, Judge James Boasberg ruled in favor of Meta, finding that the FTC failed to
show Meta currently holds monopoly power given competition from TikT ok, You T ube, and other platforms. The FTC announced in
January 2026 that it would appeal. Although Meta prevailed for now, the case remains a major structural risk since if Instagram
were ultimately spun off, Meta would lose a disproportionate share of its revenue and also the cultural relevance with younger
users.
EU REGULATORY FINES
Meta historically has faced and is currently facing a multi-front battle in Europe:
• “Pay-or-consent” model violation: In April 2025, the European Commission ruled that Meta’s pay-or-consent model
violated the Digital Markets Act (DMA), fining €200 million. Regulators found the subscription fee too high and the choice
not truly “equivalent, ” since it effectively forced users to pay for privacy. Meta agreed to update its model for Facebook and
Instagram beginning in January.
• WhatsApp AI exclusion: In February 2026, the European Commission issued a Statement of Objections alleging that Meta
breached antitrust rules by excluding third-party AI assistants from WhatsApp and favoring its own “Meta AI. ”
• Data privacy & AI training (GDPR): Under pressure from the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), the Irish Data
Protection Commission (DPC), Meta has repeatedly paused plans to use European users’ posts and photos for AI training
because of data-privacy concerns.
• Meta has also faced multiple European fines in history, including for Facebook Marketplace antitrust issues (2024) and
for unlawfully transferring EU user data to the US (2023). Unlike US litigation, which often results in one-off settlements, EU
regulatory enforcement creates ongoing operational costs that Meta must account for indefinitely.
