Not all world models are alike. Some models generate dynamic environments that 
respond to agent actions in real time, while others focus on constructing coherent 
3D worlds, predicting future observations, or learning abstract representations of 
how environments evolve. These differing approaches reflect a broader design 
spectrum - from highly generative systems that render visual worlds to more 
efficient architectures that focus on predicting latent representations of reality. 
Over time, these categories may increasingly converge as systems integrate multiple 
capabilities. 
In this note, we profile two emerging startups pursuing different approaches to 
world models: World Labs and AMI Labs.  
• World Labs, founded by Stanford professor and compute vision pioneer Fei-
Fei Li, is focused on building generative world models with spatial 
intelligence. Its current model, called Marble, aims to give AI systems a 
native understanding of three-dimensional environments, enabling coherent, 
navigable worlds that could support applications across robotics, design, and 
simulation. The company emerged from stealth in 2024 and was recently 
valued at over $5bn (according to PitchBook) following a $1bn funding round 
in February.
• AMI Labs, founded by Meta’s former Chief AI Scientist Yann LeCun, is 
pursuing a different path centered on learning efficient internal 
representations of how the world behaves. Rather than generating full visual 
environments, it focuses on models that predict high-level structure and 
dynamics in latent space, an approach designed to support reasoning, 
planning, and physical AI systems such as robotics. The company exited 
stealth earlier this month, raising >$1bn at a $4.5bn post-money valuation 
(according to PitchBook). 
World Models, Simply Explained: World models are AI systems that learn 
how environments work by building an internal representation of the world. 
In simple terms, they act like an AI “imagination engine,” allowing machines 
to simulate, explore, and reason about environments - from generating 
virtual worlds to testing actions and scenarios before acting in the real one.

World Models 101                 
What are World Models? A world model is an AI system that builds an internal, usable 
representation of an environment so it can simulate “what might happen next”, often 
including how the future changes when an agent takes actions. In today’s usage of the 
term, world models increasingly mean generative, interactive simulators: systems that can 
generate coherent video/3D scenes and keep them stable enough for exploration, editing, 
and downstream tasks like robotics training or content creation. 
• A useful mental image for non-technical readers: A world model is like an AI 
“imagination engine” that can 1) simulate and infer what the world is like now; 2) 
roll it forward in time under “what-if” conditions; and  3) expose that simulation to 
humans or agents as something you can explore, query, or edit.
Major Types of World Models: There are no universally agreed-on categories for World 
Models. However, given the wide range of different approaches, we attempt to divide into 
primary categories. Overtime, we would expect these categories to gradually blur as 
models integrate several of these capabilities - and a number of models today already do 
not fit cleanly in one category.
• Interactive Action-Conditioned World Models: These models generate 
environments that dynamically respond to an agent’s actions in real time, allowing 
AI systems to explore, plan, and learn inside simulated worlds. They behave like a 
learned game engine where the model predicts how the world changes when 
actions occur.
° Key Focus: Predicts how the environment changes given agent actions.
° Example: Google DeepMind Genie
• Coherent World Generators: These models generate coherent and persistent 3D 
environments from text, images, or video. Their focus is on constructing stable 
spatial worlds that maintain geometric consistency and can be explored or 
rendered from multiple viewpoints. Unlike action-conditioned models, they 
typically emphasize scene generation and spatial consistency rather than dynamic 
interaction.
° Key Focus: Creation of stable, navigable 3D worlds
° Example: World Labs Marble
• Abstract Representation/Non-Generative Models: These models predict highlevel representations of missing or future parts of data rather than raw sensory 
inputs like pixels. By focusing on abstract structure (objects, motion, and 
relationships) they learn how the world behaves without needing to reproduce 
every visual detail. This approach emphasizes learning the underlying structure of 
reality rather than reconstructing exact observations - overall emphasizing model 
efficiency vs. precise detail.
° Key Focus: Predicting latent world representations instead of pixels
° Example: Meta V-JEPA, AMI Labs
• Predictive Generative World Models: These models predict what the world will 
look like next, often generating the next video frame or observation. They simulate 
environmental changes but usually do not create fully interactive worlds. They are 
commonly used for physical AI planning, forecasting, and autonomous driving

    High-Level Mechanisms of a Generative/Predictive World Model
• Learn a compact internal state (latent representation): The model receives 
observations (text, images, video, sensor data) and compresses them into a 'latent 
state' - a compact internal representation that captures the relevant spatial, 
temporal, and causal structure of the environment. Rather than storing raw pixels, 
the model learns a structured representation sufficient for prediction and control.
• Predict dynamics over time: The model learns how the latent state evolves over 
time, often by predicting the next latent state (and sometimes the next 
observation). This enables it to 'roll forward' into plausible futures. Some world 
models explicitly reconstruct video observations, while others operate purely in 
latent space for planning.
• Condition on actions for 'what-if' simulation: Many practical world models 
accept actions as inputs, such as steering commands, robot motions, or game 
controls, and predict how the environment changes under different choices.
• Generate usable outputs: Depending on the system, outputs may include: 
Predicted video frames, structured 3D scenes, game states, robot motion.
• Use the model for ongoing planning and learning: Once sufficiently accurate, a 
world model allows an agent to: Evaluate candidate actions in imagination, 
compare different possible futures before acting (in a process called 'Model-Based 
Reasoning'), train policies partly or entirely inside the simulated environment, 
transfer learned behavior to reason about the real world.
• For more technical details, we recommend the following research papers:
° "World Models" by David Ha & Jürgen Schmidhuber, 2018
° "GAIA-1: A Generative World for Autonomous Driving"  by Hu, et al., 2023

Notable Challenges:
• Error Accumulation and Temporal Drift: Many world models struggle to stay 
coherent over long interactions: objects drift, geometry morphs, or even the rules 
of physics can change as the simulation runs. For example, Google DeepMind's 
Genie 3 (generally viewed as one of the most advanced generative models today) 
currently supports only a few minutes of continuous interaction. Extending 
memory horizons adds noise and can increase computational overhead and cost 
significantly. 
• Controllability: A world model may generate beautiful worlds but still offer 
limited meaningful actions (what you can reliably do inside the world) other than

• Multi-Agent and Social/Interactive Dynamics: Simulating interactions among

collecting labeled real-world sensor data can be costly and slow.
• Lack of Benchmarking Frameworks: There is currently no widely accepted 
benchmark for measuring world-model quality across long interactive horizons. As 
a result, evaluating progress in world models often relies on qualitative 
demonstrations or task-specific tests rather than standardized metrics.

 Use Cases Spanning Both Digital and Physical:
• Video Games: World models can generate fully interactive game environments 
from minimal prompts or demonstrations, enabling rapid content creation and 
dynamic world-building. This is a very topical debate for video game stocks given 
the disruption potential if others can essentially prompt a model to create their 
own video game. For views from our MS video game analyst Matthew Cost, see 
here.
• VFX/Animation: World models can generate coherent scenes that maintain object 
permanence, lighting consistency, and physical plausibility across time, reducing 
manual animation and simulation work. Artists could iteratively explore alternative 
storylines, camera movements, or scene edits by interacting directly with a 
simulated world rather than rendering frame-by-frame.
• Autonomous Vehicles: World models can simulate complex driving scenarios, 
including rare edge cases, allowing systems to evaluate decisions safely before 
deployment. They also support model-based planning, enabling vehicles to 
anticipate how traffic participants may respond to different maneuvers. AV 
companies leveraging world models today include: Tesla, Waymo, and Wayve, 
among others.
• Robotics: Robots can train inside simulated environments generated by world 
models, practicing manipulation, navigation, or coordination before operating in 
the real world. Over time, improved simulation fidelity can narrow the sim-to-real 
gap, making transfer of learned behaviors more reliable and efficient. Notable 
examples of robotics companies using world models include: 1X, Tesla, Skild AI, and 
many others.

 Notable Examples
• Genie (Google DeepMind): Genie is a generative, interactive world model that can 
transform static images or internet videos into playable, controllable 
environments. It learns environment dynamics from large-scale video data and 
allows agents to take actions inside imagined worlds. Genie 3, the latest version, 
was unveiled in August 2025, and allows users to generate multiple minutes (vs. 
10-20 seconds for Genie 2) of interactive 3D environments at a 720p resolution 
and 24 FPS. The model is also being used as the basis of the Waymo World Model 
for AV simulation. Alphabet is covered by Brian Nowak.
• V-JEPA (Meta): V-JEPA (Video-based Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture) is 
Meta’s world model approach that learns predictive dynamics from raw video to 
support planning and decision-making. The architecture is designed to support 
downstream reasoning and planning tasks without relying on full video generation 
- particularly for robotic systems which is heavily highlighted in the company's 
research papers. The latest iteration, V-JEPA 2 was unveiled in June 2025. Meta is 
covered by Brian Nowak.
• GAIA (Wayve): GAIA is a generative world model developed by UK-based startup 
Wayve for autonomous driving that predicts future driving scenes conditioned on 
vehicle actions and context. It integrates video, text, and control signals to simulate 
realistic driving scenarios for training and evaluation. The latest version, GAIA-3, 
was unveiled in December 2025 and represents a significant upgrade over its 
predecessors, with 15 billion parameters (about twice the size of GAIA-2) and 
training on roughly ten times more data.
• Cosmos (NVIDIA): Cosmos is NVIDIA’s physical AI world foundation model 
platform aimed at building large-scale, physics-aware simulators for robotics and 
autonomous systems. It combines generative modeling with simulation 
infrastructure (e.g., Omniverse) to create interactive, controllable virtual 
environments. Users include (per NVIDIA): 1X, Agile Robots, Agility Robotics, Field 
AI, Figure, Hexagon, Mentee Robotics, Skild AI, and a number of others. NVIDIA is 
covered by Joe Moore.
• Muse (Microsoft): Muse is Microsoft’s generative world model designed to learn 
the dynamics of interactive environments, particularly video games, from gameplay 
data. The model predicts how game states evolve in response to player actions, 
allowing it to generate new gameplay trajectories and simulate environments that 
respond to controls. Microsoft has positioned Muse as a tool for game 
development and AI research, where it can help generate playable game 
sequences, assist with prototyping, and support AI agents learning inside simulated 
environments. Microsoft is covered by Keith Weiss.
• Marble (World Labs):  Marble is a generative world model developed by World 
Labs focused on creating coherent, navigable 3D environments from images or 
textual descriptions. The system emphasizes spatial consistency and persistent 
geometry, enabling users to explore generated scenes from multiple viewpoints 
while maintaining stable object relationships and layout. Rather than focusing 
primarily on action-conditioned dynamics, Marble aims to construct high-quality 
spatial worlds that can serve as a foundation for interactive environments, creative 
tools, and virtual production workflows.

World Models & Physical AI                  
From our conversations with robotics experts, world models could help address 2 key 
challenges in robotics: 1) the need for large volumes of training data; and 2) enabling 
robots to reason about physical environments before acting. By learning predictive 
representations of how environments evolve over time, these models may allow robots to 
simulate potential actions and evaluate likely outcomes prior to execution, while also 
generating synthetic scenarios that expand the diversity of training data. A number of 
robotics companies are already experimenting with world models through partnerships 
with firms such as NVIDIA and Google or through internally developed systems. That said, 
in the nearer term we expect world models and simulated data to complement rather than 
replace real-world data in robotics training pipelines.
One of the primary hurdles to general-purpose AI-enabled robots remains the scale 
and diversity of training data. Collecting robotic training data in the physical world is 
expensive and time-consuming, traditionally requiring specialized teleoperation  setups  and 
constant human supervision. To train a robot on a new task, a human would need to  
teleoperate the robot to complete the exact task numerous times (potentially thousand+) 
before the robot can autonomously mimic it. World models that learn general physical 
dynamics from large-scale video or sensor data could allow robots to transfer this 
understanding across tasks and environments, potentially reducing the need for taskspecific data collection.
Several technical hurdles remain. From our conversations with robotic developers and 
researchers, most currently view world models as an enabling layer within a broader 
robotics stack rather than a standalone solution. Accurately modeling complex real-world 
physics, contact dynamics, and long-horizon interactions remains challenging, and even 
small prediction errors can compound over time in real-world deployments. Many of the 
micro-level physical details that outsiders overthink are often the most critical and also 
difficult to simulate. 
• For example: The minor forces applied to each finger as it manipulates a complex 
object,  the differences in how an actuator behaves before and after extensive 
operation, or subtle variations in surface friction and material properties that can 
cause objects to slip or move unpredictably during manipulation. Even static 
friction in robot joints can lead to large performance differences between 
simulation and reality.

About World Labs                 
What is World Labs? World Labs is an artificial intelligence startup building models that 
generate coherent interactive 3D worlds ('world models'). The goal is to help computers 
re-construct and better understand physical space (how objects fit together, move, and 
interact) much like humans do to enable new tools for entertainment, design, robotics, 
and other real-world applications - a concept described as 'spacial intelligence'. The 
company's first product, Marble, allows users to generate and interact with detailed 3D 
environments from simple inputs such as text or images.
                   Founding Story: Dr. Fei-Fei Li co-founded World Labs in 2023 after a career spanning 
academia and industry in AI and computer vision. She earned her PhD in electrical 
engineering from Caltech in 2005, then held faculty roles at the University of Illinois 
Urbana-Champaign (2005–2006) and Princeton University (2007–2009) before joining 
Stanford in 2009. 
ImageNet (work commonly dated to 2007)  introduced the project publicly in 2009. 
ImageNet organized images using a WordNet-based hierarchy and scaled to millions of 
labeled images across thousands of categories, creating a shared dataset that became 
central to computer vision research and benchmarking.
In the years that followed, progress on ImageNet benchmarks helped push the field 
toward deep learning approaches, particularly after the 2012 ImageNet Challenge, when 
AlexNet (Developed by Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton) significantly 
reduced the top-5 error rate compared with prior systems. The competition, which 
required models to classify images into 1,000 object categories and detect objects within 
complex scenes, provided a standardized way to measure performance at scale. As teams 
improved results year after year, ImageNet became closely associated with the rapid rise 
of modern computer vision, while also bringing broader attention to ongoing challenges 
such as data bias, generalization beyond benchmark datasets, and the heavy 
computational resources required to train large models.
Alongside research leadership at Stanford, Li also took on prominent organizational roles. 
She served as Chief Scientist of AI/ML at Google Cloud during a Stanford sabbatical from 
January 2017 to September 2018, and she helped found Stanford’s Institute for Human-
Centered AI in 2019. She also co-founded AI4ALL in 2017, a  nonprofit focused on 
increasing diversity in AI research.
Outside of Li, World Labs’ founding team also includes Justin Johnson, a University of 
Michigan professor known for his work in computer vision and generative models; 
Christoph Lassner, a researcher from Epic Games and Meta Reality Labs with experience in 
spacial intelligence systems; and Ben Mildenhall, a computer vision researcher from 
Google recognized for his contributions to neural radiance fields (NeRF) and 3D scene 
representation. Together, the team focuses on building AI systems centered on spatial 
intelligence and three-dimensional environments.

Marble: Marble is World Labs’ first commercial product and current flagship 'world 
model', publicly released in November 2025, that builds persistent, explorable 3D 
environments from text, still images, video, or coarse 3D inputs. It models geometry, 
depth, and object relationships to generate coherent scenes that users can navigate and 
modify. The platform enables creation and export of editable virtual worlds for 
applications in gaming, visual effects, robotics, and simulation. We outline key features 
bellow:
• Input Types: Marble can begin building a 3D world from a range of starting points, 
including text descriptions, photos, short video clips, or rough 3D layouts. This 
flexibility allows users to create environments from whatever materials they 
already have, without requiring advanced technical skills.
• World Editing: After generating a world, users can modify it by removing objects, 
adjusting details, or changing elements within the scene. This allows iterative 
refinement without rebuilding the environment from scratch.
• 'Chisel' (Sculpting): The 'chisel' function allows users to shape the basic structure 
of a scene by placing simple 3D forms such as walls or blocks before applying 
stylistic detail. It functions like creating a rough physical model first, then layering 
in texture and visual elements.
• World Expansion: Users can extend an existing scene by selecting areas and 
prompting Marble to generate additional surrounding content. This helps scale 
environments outward while maintaining visual and spatial consistency.
• Compose: Marble allows multiple previously created environments to be 
combined into a single, larger world. This makes it possible to assemble more 
complex spaces from smaller, modular scenes.
• Enhance Exported Videos: Once a world is rendered into video, Marble provides 
tools to improve visual quality and introduce motion effects such as water 
movement or atmospheric elements. These enhancements aim to make outputs 
appear more realistic and production-ready.
• Export for Downstream Projects: Generated worlds can be exported into formats 
compatible with external 3D software, game engines, and simulation tools. This 
enables use of Marble outputs within broader creative or technical workflows.
• Marble API: The Marble API allows developers to integrate world-generation 
capabilities directly into their own applications. This supports automated creation, 
editing, or scaling of virtual environments within larger software systems.

About AMI Labs                  
What is AMI Labs? Advanced Machine Intelligence (AMI) Labs is a French world model 
startup that  exited stealth in March 2026, and was founded by former researchers from 
Meta/Facebook's visual AI research team, including Yann LeCun and Alex LeBrun. While 
World Labs seeks primarily to build models that construct coherent, interactive 3D 
environments ('spacial intelligence'), AMI Labs is developing world models that learn 
abstract representations of sensor data to make efficient predictions of the real world - 
likely leveraging the JEPA framework pioneered by Meta (see more details below). Note 
that specific details of the company's planned models and use cases is limited given the 
company is so  early stage. 
Founding Story & About JEPA: Dr. Yann LeCun has been one of the central figures in the 
recent development of modern artificial intelligence, particularly in deep learning and 
computer vision. He earned his PhD in computer science from Université Pierre et Marie 
Curie (now Sorbonne University) in 1987 and later conducted influential research at Bell 
Labs during the 1990s, where he helped pioneer convolutional neural networks for tasks 
such as handwriting recognition. In the following decades he held academic positions 
including at New York University, where he remains a professor and helped establish the 
NYU Center for Data Science.
In parallel with his academic work, LeCun has played a major role in industry research. He 
joined Meta (formerly Facebook) in 2013 as Chief AI Scientist, where he led Meta’s FAIR 
(Fundamental AI Research) organization. Throughout his career he has advocated for 
approaches to AI that move beyond pattern recognition and toward systems capable of 
reasoning and understanding the world.
One of the key milestones in this vision is the development of Joint Embedding 
Predictive Architecture (JEPA). Introduced in the 2022 as part of LeCun’s broader 
proposal for “autonomous machine intelligence,” JEPA represents a shift in how models 
are trained. Instead of generating detailed outputs, JEPA models predict the latent 
representation of unseen data from surrounding context, enabling the system to capture 
higher-level semantics and relationships within the data rather than capturing every single 
detail or pixel.
• Variants such as I-JEPA (Image-based JEPA) extend this idea to visual data by 
predicting the representation of masked regions in an image from visible context. 
These models can learn meaningful semantic features without requiring labeled 
datasets and have demonstrated strong performance on downstream tasks such as 
object recognition, depth estimation, and visual reasoning. More recent work has 
expanded the framework to additional modalities. V-JEPA (Video JEPA) and related 
systems apply the same predictive architecture to video, allowing models to learn 
how scenes evolve over time and potentially enabling robots & other machines to 
anticipate actions and events in dynamic environments. 
In November 2025, LeCun announced he was leaving Meta to launch a new company 
focused on Advanced Machine Intelligence (AMI) research. AMI Labs aims to develop nextgeneration AI systems built around world models capable of learning from real-world 
sensory data such as images and video rather than relying primarily on text-based training.
