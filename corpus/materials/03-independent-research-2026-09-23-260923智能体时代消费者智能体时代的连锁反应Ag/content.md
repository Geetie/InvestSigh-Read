Revisiting Our Agentic Frameworks
We’ve been writing about the impact of AI since we began, and not just limited to the data center.
We can see the back-end, though. Despite our efforts to cover the second and third-order impacts from AI use rather than the first-order impact from its scaling, we know many of you have glossed over these in favor of reading yet another article about semis supply chain bottlenecks. That’s okay, that was the right thing to do in the first half. But now the market is a bit different.

So, rather than repeat in depth, here’s the recap:
[Original Framework] In June 2023, our second ever paid article was titled Less Deus, More Machina and detailed losers due to AI in chatbot form (with some hints at the potential for agentic disruption). Our standalone short basket heavily favored call centers, BPO, stock images, expense products, staffing agencies, legal tech and no-code website building solutions.
SPY climbed 75% since publication while our short basket dropped nearly 40%. However, many of these businesses are either extremely small now or consensus shorts at high risk of a squeeze.
The underlying question of the piece, though, remains useful…how much of a company’s economics survives when its customers can accomplish the same task more easily? Consumer agents extend that question to the effort involved in comparing, switching, negotiating and cancelling.
So far, in 2026, our coverage has been primarily centered on the impact of Agents – the shift from “who builds it” to “who uses it” . And for the most part, we’ve been looking for the beneficiaries rather than losers.
[Cybersecurity] Earlier this year the market had a conniption when it understood the second order effects of coding bots. The baby, along with the bathwater, was thrown out in a fire sale of software equities. Some of the selloff was justified, but we felt there was a large overreaction.

Our broad lines of thinking haven’t changed, but we continue to sharpen our views as this moves from theory to real world observation.
The idea is simple: agents work for consumers. Their raison d’être is to make people’s lives better by getting them the best products and the best prices, personalized to their preferences, without the hassle.
The clearest cut beneficiaries are those that either build the agents, make them better, or provide the infrastructure for them to operate. The losers are those that have no way to adapt to the new way of doing things.

Beneficiaries:
First and foremost is Meta Platforms (META US).
After months of choppy headlines, Meta’s AI team (headed up by Scale founder Alexandr Wang) finally has a consumer hit. A big one: Muse. Ten days after its launch this month, Muse reached No. 1 on Apple’s U.S. free iPhone app chart, with a 4.9 rating.
Muse is basically a personal assistant for everyone. It can remember your preferences, work across your apps, use a browser to carry out tasks and keep going after you close the app — asking for approval before sensitive actions such as purchases.

"The muse reception has honestly been beyond our biggest dreams, Wang said, on his very active X account, where he posted dozens of times in recent days highlighting the response to Muse.
With Muse, Meta has gained access to an incredible wealth of data. Your calendar, other apps, shopping habits, email. And like we said earlier this summer, Meta has the infrastructure to turn that data into dollars very quickly.
We’re seeing people use Muse not just to buy things, but to cancel things. That is extremely useful behavioral data. Meta has positioned themselves to gain insight into consumers in a manner that should take their ARPU much higher. Most of that lift will come from ads – think about how effective Meta can become at delivering ads with this incremental insight. That’s before you even consider the potential for them to work out some sort of deal where they participate in the commerce enabled by their app.
Continuing to think from the first-order, our regular partners in investigation – Hunterbrook – have turned up a beneficiary of Meta’s Muse success that is almost certainly being overlooked. Who is upstream of Meta’s success here?
Upstream Highlight: Innodata (INOD US)
Meta is Innodata’s largest customer, according to interviews with former employees and reviews of job postings. In 2024, Innodata had 400 full-time equivalent employees working on the Meta account, according to a former director. In the years since, that business has expanded.
In August, Innodata disclosed a program focused on “personalization of long-horizon agents” with its largest customer. The technical description of the project points to Muse, though neither company has confirmed the product connection. Neither Meta nor Innodata denied the relationship despite requests for comment.
Innodata’s opportunity extends beyond one app. Like Mercor and Scale AI, its offerings now span advanced training data, computer-use environments, and reusable datasets — not just conventional data labeling. And earlier this year, Innodata disclosed winning a new frontier AI lab as a customer, though more than two-thirds of its 2026 revenue so far has been attributable to its two largest customers.

Founded in 1988, Innodata spent decades doing the unglamorous work of turning documents into digitized, structured information. Today, alongside that older business, it supplies the training material, human judgment, and testing that help AI models improve. It doesn’t have to build the next great model to make money. It can get paid to help teach it.
That prospect helped turn a roughly \$4 stock in early 2023 into a \$121.50 stock at its June closing high. At the center of the transformation was a customer Innodata did not name in its financial statements. By 2025, that customer accounted for approximately \$146 million — 58% of the company’s revenue. The mystery customer was Meta.
That dependence cuts both ways. Meta’s investment in Scale raised an uncomfortable question: Why keep paying Innodata if you’ve just bought almost half of a larger competitor? The latest results added to that concern: The largest account’s quarterly revenue fell by roughly a third. Innodata’s shares are now back near \$57. The customer behind its breakout has also become its most consequential risk.
Yet interviews with former employees, public hiring records, and the company’s own disclosures point less to a customer-client relationship and more to a deeply embedded partnership. Innodata personnel have worked inside Meta’s AI organization across multiple projects. And Innodata’s new assignment from its largest customer — personalizing agents that perform long, complicated tasks — closely resembles the work behind Muse.

"With our largest customer we continue to grow as we diversify into more organizations and more Al workflows and partner with them on their flagship next-generation Al program," Innodata shared in its most recent earnings call in which the company also announced its President would be taking over as CEO."
Teaching models to carry out complicated tasks can require fresh material: expert-written answers, worked examples and simulated situations in which to practice. That demand helped take Mercor from a \$250 million valuation in 2024 to \$10 billion in 2025. Meta made an even bigger bet: \$14.3 billion for a 49% stake in Scale AI.
The strongest clue to a Muse assignment comes from Innodata itself. In August, it announced “a significant new program with our largest customer covering personalization of long-horizon agents - now scaling.”
Meta’s description of Muse Spark 1.3, which powers the assistant, sounds strikingly similar: “When working on long tasks, it adapts to user preferences.” The timing fits, too. Meta says Muse had been developed and used internally since early 2026. Innodata’s program was scaling ahead of September’s launch. The customer, task, and timing point toward Muse — or at least the shared models behind it. Neither company has publicly identified the assignment as Muserelated.
And Meta is no longer carrying the story alone. A second, unnamed Big Tech customer more than doubled its quarterly contribution to roughly \$31 million, almost matching the largest account. Innodata also disclosed winning a new frontier AI lab.

Now, Innodata is expanding to datasets designed for agentic use-cases as the company said in its August 6 press-release.
"Through our research efforts，we have established an early position in agentic reinforcement learning, one of the most important frontiers in Al development, and a second program covering reinforcement-learning environments for computer-use agentic tasks."
What, exactly, does this mean?
Innodata is now building training material for AI that acts, instead of just answering questions. The company says its simulated environments teach agents to “reason, recover from errors, and complete complex, long-horizon tasks.” This training material comes populated with fictional people, emails, documents and databases. The data is deliberately complicated and incomplete – you can think of it as a practice version of the messy digital life that an AI assistant will be eventually asked to manage.
Consider an instruction as simple as “plan my trip to Paris.” An assistant like Muse might need to check your calendar, compare flights, find a hotel, arrange transportation, and ask for approval before spending your money.
Along the way, it might notice that an old email lists dates you have since changed. It also might recall your preference for nonstop flights. It’ll also help distinguish the restaurant that your friend had recommended instead of the one they told you to avoid.
These are the kinds of challenges behind Meta’s promise of an assistant that works across applications and remembers what matters
![006_p011_05.jpg](figures/006_p011_05.jpg)

Some parts have checkable answers: Did the agent select the right dates? Stay within budget? Obtain permission? Innodata says it builds automated checks for those kinds of outcomes.
But other decisions depend on judgment. Is saving \$100 worth a sixhour layover? Does “somewhere lively” mean a neighborhood full of restaurants — or a room above a nightclub? A reservation can be perfectly valid and still be a terrible choice. The challenge is not merely teaching the assistant to book a trip. It is teaching it to understand what a good trip means to the person asking.
That is where human judgment becomes training data. Innodata says its reviewers score AI responses and explain their mistakes, following guidelines intended to “objectify subjectivity.”
For a company like Meta, the potential value is an assistant that learns when to act, when to ask, and when mission accomplished is not quite the same as a job well done.

What’s the first reaction that companies will have to all of this? Easy, they' Il fight back. They will do whatever they can to ensure that humans, rather than machines, are the ones that have to do the things they’re relying on humans being too lazy to do. They won’t go quietly into that night of LTV decline. And one company is positioned to benefit from all of their efforts.
It’s not like these merchants can simply ban all agents, also. They need to distinguish an authorized customer representative from scraping, fraud or abusive automation. Cloudflare already works with Visa and Mastercard on agent commerce. The commercial question is whether this produces additional paid security and infrastructure usage.

We’ve gotten a few things right in the above, as far as market price action is concerned, and a few things incorrect. But we believe that going forward, we’ll require a new framework that goes beyond simple impact and adaptability and focuses more on the pricing power of the winners and the risks of the losers.
The losers are the companies who provide similar marketplace services and therefore are at risk of disintermediation, as well as companies clinging to excess economics through excessive friction costs.
![013_p019_12.jpg](figures/013_p019_12.jpg)

Tedium rent is predicated on the fact that people are impatient. Most of us would rather eat the cost of an unused subscription than have to pick up the phone, wait on hold for several minutes, and then listen to a customer service rep try to dissuade us from cancelling.
Fortunately for the consumer, agents now have telephony capabilities. They don’t feel the effects of linear time and are willing to wait on hold indefinitely, even withstanding that horrible elevator music. As far as we know, sales scripts aren’t going to persuade an AI, so those barriers to exit are now demolished.
Inertia rent works because people are lazy. We are content with letting unused subscriptions renew because the alternative would mean having to jump through the requisite hoops to cancel or shop for a new rate. The agent’s natural state, by contrast, is to be hyperaware – constantly evaluating your expense footprint to find redundancies and inefficiencies.
Attention rent is a blend of inertia and tedium. We are inundated with ads for things that we don’t think about 99% of the time. But, we have finite attention spans and are prone to recency bias. We make purchases based on convenience – which speaks to the potency of big brand advertising.
Machines have no habits, and aren’t influenced by ads. They will shop for the best deals, and can objectively evaluate several alternatives simultaneously.
The Agent Kills Tedium First and Attention Last
![014_p020_13.jpg](figures/014_p020_13.jpg)

Finally, asymmetry rent is synonymous with aggregation. The platform knows more than the customer does, meaning they are entitled to a cut of the transaction for the effort of orchestration. A Finder ’ s Fee, if you will.
Of course, when information is commodified, that asymmetry withers, and aggregation is less important. An agent with access to that information can upend incumbent aggregator models in just a few prompts.
We anticipate that agents will begin to cut in on margins for companies that have built up their moat around rent-seeking on top of tedium, inertia, attention and/or asymmetry.

The math might work that killing a flock of golden geese is bad for the egg-laying business.
Companies that are screwed here are the ones that help consumers make better personal finance (or shopping) decisions.

Financial Data
While an agent can explain your credit report, find a better loan and help you apply, the lender still needs to know whether you’ll repay it. That’s not going to be disintermediated by AI anytime soon, in our view.
But it is eminently possible that the price of financial guidance can fall while demand for the information increases.
Equifax (EFX US) and Experian (EXPN LN) sit underneath many of the services consumers interact with. Their databases, data-provider relationships and verification capabilities supply information that a more capable model cannot simply generate. As agents make it easier to compare financial products and complete applications, there is an opportunity for additional paid credit checks, income verifications and fraud screening. The consumer may change how they shop while the lender continues buying the same underlying information.
Credit Karma provides Equifax and TransUnion reports for free, then typically earns a commission when a customer is approved for a recommended product. Gen Digital also uses Equifax data across its identity-protection and financial offerings. We can therefore own a supplier while shorting businesses whose economics depend more heavily on controlling the consumer relationship.

For Gen, the pressure comes from a customer who now has help scrutinizing every renewal. Norton and LifeLock have useful features, but an agent can identify overlapping coverage, compare alternatives and help the customer switch. The services can remain useful while the price consumers are willing to pay for the package declines.
Intuit faces a different version of the same problem. Credit Karma’s free service monetizes the journey from financial information to a product recommendation. If an agent takes over that journey, referral economics become more contested. An application routed away from Credit Karma can still produce revenue for a bureau. TurboTax adds exposure to the declining cost of financial administration: gathering documents and navigating a cheaper preparation service become less daunting when the customer has assistance.
Experian also has an opportunity to verify the agents themselves. Its announced Agent Trust framework links verified consumers to agents acting on their behalf, with collaborators including Visa, Cloudflare and Skyfire. That remains a developing commercial opportunity, but it gives us a concrete example of agent adoption creating another use for identity information.
There are crosscurrents. Experian has consumer subscriptions and marketplaces of its own. QuickBooks’ accounting records and embedded workflows give Intuit a more durable business, and Intuit is already distributing its products through Claude. Our bet is that verified data retains more pricing power as the consumer interface changes.
Retail
The disaggregation mechanism will also unfold across the retail ecosystem. Companies like Uber (UBER US) and Instacart (CART US) are textbook aggregators – packaging a collection of non-exclusive suppliers (drivers, grocers). Their business model banks on the fact that people are either too impatient or too lazy to go through the process of evaluating their options, making a decision, calling the restaurant or visiting the store, and making the transaction.

We are simply highlighting this reasoning again - UBER, CART volumes probably go unchanged as people go from using the apps to using agents…but that could impact advertising revenue in the short term. Aggregators
Aggregators have also operated under a static assumption: information asymmetry between buyers and sellers warrants a “take rate” from whoever runs the platform. It’s a fair proposition, but frankly, the fees have gotten out of hand.
NerdWallet (NRDS US) is the textbook definition of this model. The company hosts a variety of financial services products on their platform – ranging from consumer lending, auto insurance, savings accounts, and mortgages. Crucially, they are not the ones underwriting or offering any of these products. That task is instead undertaken by the companies who advertise on their platform. In exchange, NerdWallet generates sales on ad interactions, clicks, and impressions.
NerdWallet is joined by LendingTree (TREE US), EverQuote (EVER US), MediaAlpha (MAX US), and QuinStreet (QNST US) in employing this model. Performance marketing extracts the asymmetry rent – or the fact that consumers simply aren’t always equipped with the best information when making a decision. The proliferation of agentic distribution makes that risk a little more tangible. As agents eschew the marketplace model in favor of going direct to the source, that works against the performance marketing model upon which the internet’s earliest winners were built on.
As Agentic traffic replaces human, these networks start to decay. Information gets old, accounts get stale, and the company’s value as an intermediary dissipates.
