A Morgan Stanley Research Key Theme of 2026
Open-weights models have become increasingly important to the AI narrative.
The open vs. closed-weights model debate has reached a fever pitch over the past weeks with 1) the release of Kimi K3, 2) potential US government restrictions of Chinese models, and 3) the "Open Weights and American AI Leadership" and Anthropic letters. Investors are concerned that compute and AI infrastructure demand will fall with the proliferation of smaller, more efficient open-weight Chinese models. We believe open-weights models are good for competition and can enable faster AI diffusion, supported by Jevons Paradox. This is important given we see a long runway for enterprise AI adoption (see math showing an average AI task provides a $55 benefit vs. $2-5 cost here). The big question is: Who wins — open or closed-weights models — and what does the world look like in each scenario?
Many enterprises are using both closed and open-weights models today. According to a McKinsey survey, 63% of surveyed companies use open-weights models today in combination with closed ones. The Debate on Open-Weights Models
Overview of Open-Weights Models
We believe that the market fundamentally misunderstands the role and functionality of open-weights models. Historically, open-weights models tend to be smaller (in number of total parameters), faster, and more cost-efficient; as such, the wider audience came to expect these models to be of "lower quality" or "light" versions of the higher-parameter frontier models (known as closed-weight models). The difference between open-weights (or open-weight) models and closed weight models has nothing to do with the number of active parameters, overall cost, or efficiency. The distinction between the two relates to how users pay and/or interact with the models: open-source models are 1) free to access, as opposed to gated by subscriptions, 2) able to be trained and tuned (weights are "open" or able to be changed), as opposed to be the same for every user (weights are "closed"), and 3) give ultimate deployment flexibility, as it can be run on-prem, in the cloud, or through an API (either model creator's API, hosted service like AWS Bedrock, or self-hosted API; outlined in Exhibit 2 ). Closed-weight models, alternately, can either be accessed directly from a model provider (the user pays a licensing fee, like a subscription, for access, and a token cost that serves as a proxy for GPU usage) or through a cloud provider should PII be a concern.
So: what are they? Put simply: open-source models are simply models that can be adapted to a specific use case (provided a user has sufficient time, money, and compute) while closed weight models are the same for every user. Open-weight or closed-weight is not necessarily a permanent state, rather describing the permissions of a user's access. The highest-performing models, like those produced by the frontier labs, are closed weight because of the incredible monetary costs with producing them. What is the difference between open-weights and open source models? Open-weight models release their trained parameters but may withhold training data, code, or unrestricted licensing. Examples include Meta’s Llama, Google's Gemma, Qwen, DeepSeek, and Kimi (under a strict definition). Open-source models release the broader stack under open licenses, often including weights, code, training guidelines, and data details. Examples include AI2’s OLMo.
Distribution of open-source models is another key debate. Releasing a strong open-source model wins market share and a customer base while better enabling the spread of technology (which benefits all players). Some labs release open-source models — or progressions in model architecture — in alignment with academic research, where progressions in the field benefit all players. Others hypothesize that the release of models like K3 or Qwen 3.8 are deliberate attempts to undermine frontier labs' credibility; prior to models like Qwen, frontier labs showed clear advantages in model efficiency. Until the release of K3, closed-weight frontier models were seen as being clear leaders in accuracy/ benchmarking. This dynamic is a part of nascent market where we see multiple labs jockey for position, competing on model metrics like price, accuracy, benchmarking, speed, overall efficiency, and safety.
What Does Enterprise Adoption of Open-Weights Models Look Like Today?
Open-weights models are heavily used as part of companies' AI model tech stacks. According to a McKinsey survey of 700 tech leaders across 41 countries, 63% of respondents use open source models, often alongside closed options like the frontier labs. Recent stats indicate US companies have routed more than 30% of their OpenRouter tokens to Chinese open models every week from February to July 2026 (although this likely skews towards startups than enterprises). While we still think open-weights models account for the minority of LLM usage at enterprises, this shows that companies are more aggressively experimenting with an open/closed hybrid approach: it's important to understand how the mix between open and closed-weights models could shift over time.
We'd cite a few key benefits of open-weights models to enterprises:
1. Eliminate Token Costs (but this doesn't mean it's free). Given customers of open-source pay nothing in upfront costs (most, if not all, open-weights models are free), they are then free to call it as many times as they want at zero cost. Please see Exhibit 2 for an explanation of potential costs of open- and closedweight models. Open-weight models still require hosting costs or physical compute, plus the cost of training, so it's not accurate to call them free on a TCO basis. However, open-weights models could help lower opex burdens and mitigate ballooning per-token inference costs — particularly useful for use cases with high query volume. We would caveat that 1) this analysis was done in December 2025; 2) not all open-weights models are cheaper on a per-token basis, as we've seen with Kimi K3; and 3) there are other costs associated with open-weights models (more below). 2. Customization. Fine-tuning a model to industry standards or company's internal data (like customer support logs, internal documents, domain-specific tasks) can be highly effective from a performance standpoint, which can be key for highly specialized or repetitive tasks on the same data but costly to execute depending on the scale of training/fine-tuning.
3. Control Over Hosting. With the increasing importance of data sovereignty and localization rules, open-weights models give companies control over where to host their models (on-prem, private cloud, etc.). A survey from McKinsey showed that 40% of enterprise leaders like open-weights models for this reason. This is particularly beneficial for highly regulated industries with local hosting requirements.
But there are also a few drawbacks:
1. Cost / Time of Fine-Tuning Models to Stay at the Leading Edge. Fine-tuning an open-source model incurs additional costs that scale, unsurprisingly, on the amount of compute necessary. While this is increasingly easy with coding tools, it can become onerous for enterprises who want to stay on the leading edge as they must re-tune open-weight models as new versions of the base model are released. We believe enterprise readiness and in-house talent are key bottlenecks, as less tech-enabled companies don't have the resources to do this and will likely use an API for faster time to value.
2. Potential Security Risks. Bad actors can train the safety alignment — the process that instructs AI models to refuse harmful requests — out of open-weights models, posing risks for enterprise adopters. Once released, weights are beyond the developer's control and modifications are hard to change. We've seen examples of how this can be utilized — in April, the Financial Times and Alice (AI safety group) used Heretic to remove safety guards from open-weights models from Meta and Google in minutes. On the flip side, some argue that operating with a small number of closed models creates more risk or a "single point of failure."
3. Liability Attribution Issues for Enterprises. When companies access closed source, commercial models from players like OpenAI and Anthropic, they receive IP indemnities that protect them from copyright infringement claims arising from using that model provider (conditional on safety guardrails and correct usage). However, this is not the case with open-weights models, which could create risk for enterprises.
4. Geopolitical Risk. Enterprises care about business continuity. Potential bans of Chinese open-weights models in the US, as well as frontier models in China create risk for global businesses. This could cause companies in the US to avoid Chinese open-weights models and opt for US alternatives.

So, where and how are open-weights models being used for today? Primarily smaller deployments requiring speed, customization, or frequent calls. The use cases we hear enterprises using open-weights models for include: 1) coding, 2) document parsing, and 3) other smaller, specialized use cases. These are more isolated use cases, but we believe this can change as open-weights models proliferate. Today, we believe most companies either 1) use a standard enterprise offering from a model provider, or 2) partner directly with a frontier-model provider to customize those models. This allows companies to stay on the frontier and eliminate unnecessary engineering overhead.
As mentioned above, companies have full flexibility over where they run open-weights models – in the cloud, on-prem, or through an API. For companies using open-weights models for data security reasons, one option is to run those workloads on-prem, requiring them to buy or lease GPU clusters. Research from Carnegie Mellon indicates that payback periods for open-weights models vastly ranges by deployment size, with payback periods ranging from up to 3 months for small deployments (<30B parameter model, used for customer support, document analysis, etc.) to up to 6 years for large deployments (200B-1T parameter model, used for advanced research or enterprise-wide generative applications). This illustrates how open-weights models are not necessarily "free," depending on what an enterprise is using them for.
We believe orchestration and observability will become increasingly important, as open-weights models proliferate. Running LLMs in production requires a broader software and infrastructure stack, regardless of whether an enterprise uses closed or open-weight models. The importance of these layers depends less on the model’s licensing structure and more on the number of models and workflow complexity. A few key layers include gateway layer (provides a standardized access point to models and manages authentication, rate limits, logging, retries, and provider failover), routing layer (selects appropriate model for a given request, given cost, capability, latency, etc.), orchestration layer (coordinates multi-step reasoning, turns requests into a series of model/tool calls), and observability layer (tracks token usage, evals, quality, latency, and cost), among others. A shift toward open-weight models would likely increase both the number of models enterprises deploy and workload complexity – making routing, orchestration, observability, security, and governance increasingly important parts of the enterprise AI stack.
We don't believe open-weights reflects close to the majority of enterprise AI model spend, but how this trends will be important to watch. In addition to the fine-tuning required to stay at the frontier, switching costs, brand/trust, and aversion to foreign models are also key adoption hurdles. We also believe there are broader misconceptions with open-weights models around data privacy; once a user downloads an reliable open-source model, the model providers lose any insight into the users introduced training data or modified model weights unless that training is done through the provider's hosted API (in which case, uploaded data and information is in the possession of the provider, who may or may not use that data, keep it isolated from other customers, or encrypt it). Protecting one's data becomes an issue of cybersecurity or potential distillation attacks. Overall, shifts of enterprise model spend are subject to change as we see greater model efficiency, developments in open-weights models, and enterprise purchasing behavior.

Given it's still yet to be seen how the mix of open and closed weights model trends for enterprises, we believe it's useful to frame three potential scenarios we see playing out over the next few years:
1. Closed-Weights Models Win. Frontier-model performance remains difficult to replicate, which leads to a small number of well-capitalized labs winning. Enterprises prioritize accuracy, reliability, security, indemnification and ease of deployment over model control or transparency, and they choose to partner with the labs rather than fine-tune open-weights models in-house. AI workloads remain concentrated in hyperscaler clouds, and training demand remains highly compute-intensive. Cost per token falls as models become more efficient, but the cost of compute remains higher than it is in the open-weight scenario given the market oligopoly. Security software is a key winner, but more of the security burden is handled by the frontier labs vs. on-prem. Further, the massive electricity demand of training superclusters further exacerbate the demand for reliable, GW scale power: bullish for onsite power providers.
