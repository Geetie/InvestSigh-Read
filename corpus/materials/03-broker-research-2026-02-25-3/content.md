Executive Summary
Areas recently perceived to be disrupted by AI are generally characterized as services + cyclical. A few takeaways:
1. The subset of the services + cyclical group that has seen outsized underperformance recently is actually a fairly small percentage of S&P 500 market cap (13%), which helps to explain the relatively limited pullback in major indices thus far.
2. These areas are now under-owned (20th percentile of net exposure levels back to 2010) and cheap (9th percentile of historical relative valuation levels).
3. Importantly, these cohorts have both high AI adopter concentration and strong pricing power (top third of groups) based on our thematic team's AI mapping analysis.
4. Our work shows that performance dispersion remains elevated in related pockets of the market (>90th percentile back to 2010 for many groups), which means a stock-specific approach continues to make sense, in our view.

In our view, what’s going on now is typical of a major investment cycle. Volatility bands tend to widen and there are intermittent periods along the way where markets question both the pace of capital spending and which areas of the market could be disrupted. Disruption Risks: Framing the Debate from Both Sides
Stephen Byrd
How Disruptive is AI to Business Models? This has become the central question that investors are asking. The sources of potential disruption fall into 2 categories: (1) risk that significant AI-driven deflation will cause product prices (and potentially margins) to fall dramatically, and (2) risk that a new competitor will develop a new, cheaper way to perform the service at a lower cost and/or higher level of performance/quality. Examples cited recently include logistics optimization, new marketplaces designed to eliminate middlemen, agentic AI that would bypass typical commerce channels, and new software products developed by small teams.
Stepping back, at the heart of the AI disruption debate is a technology that is improving at a non-linear rate, which presents challenges to assessing impacts given the dramatic "duration mismatch" between the rate of AI capability growth and the long duration implied in most equity valuations. Humans do not naturally/easily think in non-linear dimensions. This is evident with respect to investor dialogue on the future implications of ~10x the magnitude of compute used to train the next generation of American Large Language Models (LLMs). If the LLM training scaling laws hold in 2026, which we believe is likely to be the case, we expect to see several US Frontier LLMs introduced in 1H26 with capabilities far in excess of the current models. In our Thematic note outlining 10 Predictions for 2026 that we published in January, Prediction #10 has taken center stage faster than we expected: "Transformative AI" Will Drive Deflation, Higher Capex, Changes in Asset Valuations and National Competitiveness. Are AI Disruption Fears Premature?
Andrew Pauker and Michelle Weaver
Heightened focus on AI disruption has led to significant underperformance in certain services areas of the market, while investor concerns around the pace of AI capex and labor force reduction have lingered. While we're respectful of structural risks on these fronts (see above), we think there are some contradictory elements to these dynamics, especially in the sense that they all seem to be in focus at the same time.
For instance, if AI is going to be highly disruptive and far-reaching in its effectiveness, doesn’t that support the investment case for the enablers/hyperscalers/infrastructure providers, especially since the market is broadly rewarding high capex/sales (see our factor work), the cost of capital is contained, the cost of compute is still elevated, and we haven't seen valuation excess in the mega cap enablers (Mag 7 multiple is down since 2023)? Furthermore, if AI is likely to lead to more job loss, isn’t there a phase-in period where natural attrition isn’t replaced, efficiencies are realized and margins expand? Finally, to get realization of efficiencies, don’t we first need the broader buildout of the agentic application layer and the subsequent workflow integration to occur at the enterprise level?
In our view, what’s going on right now is typical of a major investment cycle. Volatility bands tend to widen and there are intermittent periods along the way where markets question both the pace of capital spending and which areas of the market could be disrupted. Dispersion is also elevated, which is typical as the market contemplates relative leaders/laggards. This relative preference can also flip back and forth. What’s somewhat unique about this capex boom, particularly relative to the late 1990s, is that today we’re in an early cycle backdrop—specifically in terms of earnings as we’ve come off the rolling recession of 2022-2025. Thus, as capital is rotating out of certain areas, it’s not just finding its way into structural leaders, its also finding its way into cyclical leaders. This is why the equal weighted S&P has broadly outperformed. This trend is ongoing as we’ve written about extensively, driven by the strongest earnings growth for the median stock in 4 years and the return of positive operating leverage.

What Have Past Technology Shifts Meant for the Job Landscape?
AI is not the first sweeping technological change to transform the job landscape. Over the past 150 years, technological innovations from electrification to the internet have brought about significant changes to the way we work and the types of roles that are needed. Electrification enabled more efficient factories displacing steam-powered roles but created demand for electricians and engineers. The invention of the tractor resulted in the mechanization of farming, reducing the need for manual labor while fueling industrial growth which in turn spurred migration to cities. Computers and word processing tools streamlined administrative tasks, cutting back on administrative roles but giving rise to a new, highly paid profession: computer programming. Spreadsheets automated financial modeling, significantly freeing up analysts' time. The internet and e-commerce disrupted traditional retail, eliminating some jobs while creating new ones in programming, digital marketing, and web development.

Each wave of technological transformation has brought both disruption and opportunity, reshaping work across sectors. We looked at which jobs gained or lost the most share every decade and the technological shifts described above are evident. During the 1990s, computer scientists/programmers saw the biggest share increase in the total number of employees while secretaries and bookkeepers, accountants, and auditing clerks were among those seeing the biggest share decrease. This reflects the widespread adoption of the computer along with word processing software and spreadsheets. A similar pattern was observed in the 2000s and the 2010s. However, in the 2010s retails salespeople and cashiers joined the list of the top 10 roles seeing the biggest decline in shares. This is likely due to greater adoption of e-Commerce as online shopping was made easier with the rise of smartphones. We expect AI to do the same. While some roles may be automated, others will see enhancement through AI augmentation and other, entirely new roles will be created.

How is the infrastructure for the Agentic Commerce ecosystem being built?
We think a useful way to make sense of the future “agentic commerce infrastructure” is to separate the existing payments pipes (which we think will remain largely unchanged) from the new "agent control layer" being built on top. Agentic commerce does not replace the existing payments execution layer (checkout to acquirer to network to issuer, etc.). Instead it changes how the payment is initiated and how credentials and consent are represented. While it is likely technically feasible for AI agents to interact with merchants and (at least partially) complete ecommerce transactions today, what is clear is that in order for this channel to scale, common standards or protocols – as well as new commercial front-ends – must be established in order for the numerous parties within the ecommerce/payments ecosystem to communicate and work together in a secure, robust, and efficient manner. The new piece, which we refer to as the "agent control layer" standardizes how an AI agent operates and interacts with the various other parties of the ecommerce ecosystem. This is how an AI agent:
• gets permission (mandate/consent),
• proves who it is (agent identity/attestation, reputation),
• exchanges order state (cart, shipping, taxes),
• and obtains payment capability (tokenized credentials).
We break down the various key pieces of this new layer below:
1) Agentic Commerce Surfaces: This represents the "front door" to agentic commerce, the UI which shoppers interact with, and where the agent runs. Examples of this includes OpenAI's Instant Checkout, as well as merchant specific agents/front-ends, such as Walmart's Sparky.

Currently, there are multiple protocols being designed by a number of different leading industry players such as Visa, Mastercard, Google, Stripe and others, which aim to create a robust system to enable agentic commerce for merchants. Mainly, the protocols developed by these players serve three key functions: 1) Verifying agent identity (while differentiating from bots) and authenticity of intent to transact; 2) Securely exchanging payment credentials, and 3) Executing transactions (orchestration) across rails in a transparent/traceable way.
In general, we do not believe that a single protocol, developed by a single party will govern agentic payments on the web; rather, we expect the ecosystem to be composed of multiple, often open and interoperable protocols/standards that work together to deliver secure agentic commerce use cases. Against this backdrop, we think it is helpful to outline some of the agentic protocols developed so far – considering what problem within the agentic commerce ecosystem each seeks to address and how they fit together.
3) Trusted Agent Protocol (TAP) – “Can I trust this agent?” TAP is a network-led protocol from Visa focused on agent legitimacy and verification. Its purpose is to help merchants and payment networks distinguish trusted AI agents from bots or malicious automation. TAP provides standards for agent identification and attestation, giving merchants confidence that an incoming interaction is from a recognised, authorised agent rather than an unknown script. TAP does not define how checkout works or how payment is executed. Instead, it addresses the trust and provenance problem at the edge of agentmerchant interaction.
4) Agent Payments Protocol (AP2) – “Is this agent allowed to pay?” AP2 is a payments focused protocol designed to standardise delegated payment authority. It defines how an agent can cryptographically prove that it has been granted permission by a user to make a payment within defined constraints (for example, spend limits, merchants, or categories). AP2 is best understood as a mandate and authorisation layer, not a checkout protocol. It is intentionally payment-rail-agnostic and could, in principle, be used with cards, account-to-account payments, or other rails. Its role is to give merchants and payment providers verifiable assurance that a payment request is consistent with the user’s intent.
5) Agentic Commerce Protocol (ACP) – “How does the agent actually check out?” ACP is a commerce interaction protocol that standardises how agents and merchants communicate during shopping and checkout. It defines structured exchanges for product discovery, cart building, pricing, shipping, confirmation, and payment initiation. ACP sits above payments execution. It does not issue credentials or authorise transactions. Instead, it provides a common “checkout language” so that agents (such as ChatGPT) can integrate with many merchants without bespoke logic. Payments are still processed by the merchant’s chosen PSP or acquirer. While ACP launched alongside Stripe and integrates easily for Stripe merchants, it is not exclusive to Stripe. Merchants retain control of their payments backend, and the protocol is designed to be processor-agnostic.

AI Disruption Fact vs Fiction
Freight Transportation is likely one of the most exposed industries to AI disruption, but the nature of that disruption varies meaningfully between near-term overreaction and longer-term structural risk. Transportation screens as one of the sectors with potential AI-driven savings exceeding 100% of 2026 estimated pretax earnings, reflecting both high labor intensity and operational complexity. AI is already automating timeconsuming processes like freight classification, shipment planning, invoice validation, and tracking. For example, C.H. Robinson has reduced LTL classification time from ~10 minutes to ~10 seconds using AI agents, and predictive tools are helping companies cut forecasting errors and lost sales materially. Our November 2025 survey of ~200 brokers, shippers, and carriers shows adoption is already mainstream (54% using AI; another 33% planning to adopt), with most companies expecting modest but positive impact beginning now. In that sense, AI-driven efficiency gains are real and ongoing.

However, structural gains from Gen AI and Agentic AI dropping through to the bottom line may be overstated. Large brokers are rapidly deploying similar tools, suggesting these capabilities are becoming “table stakes” rather than durable competitive moats. In an industry that monetizes information asymmetry between shippers and carriers, widespread AI adoption could democratize pricing and matching data, compressing margins over time. Yet history suggests that many software innovations (e.g., EDI to API transitions, mobile app rollouts) ultimately became standardized rather than structurally disruptive. Even smaller brokers are already adopting off-the-shelf AI tools, reducing the likelihood of a winner-take-all dynamic. As a result, much of the Gen AI excitement may translate into competed-away efficiency rather than lasting excess returns. While some may push back against the notion of commoditized AI and point out that having scale and a historical dataset is unique to the large players and will help them win the AI race over time, we counter that a) having a historical data/network advantage has not prevented incumbents in other sectors from being disrupted by tech startups, b) AI can drive vertical integration of the 3PL function up into BCOs or down into Shippers, many of whom have their own dataset advantages, and c) the digitization of 3PL in the last decade has already reduced the “relationship” advantage in favor of automation.
Indeed, several 3PL stocks were down 5% over the last week (and as much as 20% intra-day on February 12th) making Transports only the latest sector to be roiled by AI disintermediation fears that initially targeted Software stocks. This was exacerbated by media reports and social media references to startup AI companies creating full-fledged brokerage operations in weeks, at a fraction of the typical investment. The concern is that rapid gains with AI capabilities could effectively disintermediate companies in the middle of the value/supply chain, particularly asset-light brokers/intermediaries. Unlike other areas where these fears may be overblown, we believe the caution is at least somewhat warranted in the case of 3PLs/brokers. As such, the more substantive longterm disruption lies in Physical AI (particularly autonomous trucking and robotics). AI Adoption Beneficiaries
On net, we believe most Freight Transportation subsectors are more likely to be beneficiaries of AI adoption than outright victims of disruption, but the balance differs meaningfully by business model. Asset-heavy operators (truckload, LTL, rails, and warehouse operators) appear positioned to capture more durable upside. AI-driven route optimization, predictive maintenance, demand forecasting, and network planning directly lower operating ratios and improve asset utilization. More importantly, Physical AI (autonomous trucking and humanoid robotics) has the potential to structurally reduce labor costs (a major portion of linehaul and warehouse expense) and expand margins in a way that is harder to commoditize. Companies with dense networks, scale, and capital access to deploy autonomous trucks or warehouse automation (e.g., large carriers, rails, contract logistics providers) are therefore likely to see AI benefits outweigh disruption risks over time.

By contrast, asset-light 3PLs and brokers face a more balanced risk/reward and in some cases greater disruption exposure. Gen AI and Agentic AI are rapidly becoming “table stakes,” with 65% of brokers already using AI tools and most reporting only modest productivity gains. 1. Asset-Heavy TL & LTL Carriers (KNX, SNDR, WERN, SAIA, XPO, ODFL, JBHT)

Bottom Line: The most defensible names are those protected by physical scarcity (networks, terminals, fleets), embedded customer integration, and proprietary operational data. Asset-light intermediaries (e.g., traditional brokers) face greater risk of GenAI-driven commoditization, while asset-heavy operators and Physical AI leaders are better positioned to convert AI from disruption risk into structural advantage.
