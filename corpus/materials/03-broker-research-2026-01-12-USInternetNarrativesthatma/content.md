The Internet is a story, and we the collective story-tellers. And 2025 isn’t winning any Pulitzer prizes. The story lines sound like a poor daytime soap - AI villains waking up as heroes, episodes around China, tariffs, courtroom dramas, and financing schemes all in such quick succession that we’d lost the plot. And with these story arcs came violent, torque-driven stock moves that even us authors seemed to be chasing, not writing, the story. PM SUMMARY — 4 THEMES THAT MATTER IN 2026
THEME #1: AI GOES FROM MODEL PERFORMANCE → PRODUCT USAGE… AND REVENUE GENERATION
The biggest debate on the consumer AI front in 2H25 was Google vs. OpenAI (private) vying for the consumer AI crown. User numbers remain infrequent KPIs and difficult to compare by design, and thus we’ve defaulted to model performance benchmarking. While performance clearly matters in the race for the best frontier model, investor patience is running quite thin for yet another capital-intensive training cycle to score basis points better on an SAT for LLMs without accompany usage acceleration. In 2026, we expect investor focus to shift from dissecting model performance towards AI product usage, the repeatable usefulness of promised AI capabilities, and an increasing focus on financial returns across ROIC, revenues, and cost savings. When can we expect the pivot from usage to monetization is TBD, but when we get there expect the violence of the focus shift to be swift and extreme. We see four components to this transition:
Recurring engagement metrics take center stage. You don’t have a consumer Internet business without eyeballs - everything is the attention economy. But window shopping isn’t the same as being a good customer. Google and Meta have shared monthly AI chatbot users numbers (MAU), while OpenAI has gone a step further with a weekly user KPI (WAU). Expect investor focus to shift to (1) continued growth of the WAU or MAU denominator, and (2) a daily user number (DAU) a proxy for the depth of engagement looking at a DAU/WAU or DAU/MAU ratio.
• Third-party integrations need more than headlines. Commercial use cases and engagement will build off integrations with existing apps and possibly new ones. We’ve started to see initial deployments (e.g., Etsy, Walmart (covered by Zhihan Ma), Shopify (not covered), Booking (covered by Richard Clarke), Zillow, Instacart, DoorDash etc.) but there’s a long way to go on improving the experience and changing consumer behavior. These partnerships dominated 2025 headlines, but for the participating companies to benefit in 2026 we’ll need to see evidence of user traction acceleration. If user behavior starts to shift, expect participating companies to benefit, if not, broader "AI winners” may get penalized and smaller at-risk Internet “AI laggards” may outperform.
OpenAI ROIC sets up 2027+ baseline expectations for the sector. Be it advertising, affiliate fees, take rate, or all of the above, OpenAI has the unenviable task of not only executing on the engagement and 3P deliverables, but also needs to offer investors evidence on the viability of the \~\$200B 2030 revenue target as proxy for sector-wide ROIC. Which means in 2026 we won’t just care about engagement but also about delivering financial returns. Meta may well be the dark horse here as even though their frontier model lags peers, their ability to drive engagement and have the model power ROIC-generating initiatives may be second-to-none.
CapEx dollars grow, but intensity peaks. Monetization is one side of the ROIC equation. Model efficiency and capital intensity also drive the narrative. As expected, all the major players guided to continued CapEx growth in 2026, albeit with limited detail. We expect a deceleration vs. 2025, but the dollar growth should still be strong off a bigger base. This likely continues into 2027 given where we are in the AI cycle, but capital intensity (as % of Revenue) could peak in 2026 and start tapering in 2027 to the benefit of ROIC, barring any unexpectedly high CapEx raises in 2027.
THEME #2: AI GETS REAL (WORLD)
If 2025’s big tech debate was whether AI eats software, then 2026 is likely to mark a shift towards AI progress in the physical realm. We are mostly thinking about Autonomous Vehicles (“AVs”) as we look out to 2027/28, but robotics and automation will become a bigger driver of efficiency gains in 2026. Wearables will test product-market fit.
Autonomous Vehicles hit the road in more markets. Between Waymo, Tesla (not covered), and the various AV companies that Uber and Lyft are working with globally, robotaxis will ramp. 2026 will lay the groundwork, with 2027 and 2028 likely to be when AVs scale, as we go from a few thousand cars across the US and EMEA to tens of thousands of cars. For the rideshare platforms, it’s existential to participate in the go-to-market plans — we think they can improve fleet economics. With more bearish competitive scenarios getting priced into the multiple, Uber risk/reward looks more attractive again.
• Robotics as a source of competitive advantage. Amazon is the best way to play this theme, with room to drive gross margin efficiency gains across warehouse automation, logistics, and fulfillment. If the past few years was all about minimizing the miles traveled per widget from manufacturing facility to your front door, then 2026+ marks a shift towards minimizing human touchpoints across the journey - from automated manufacturing, autonomous long-haul and last mile delivery, and robotics-heavy 3PL services.

THEME #3: GROWING PIES AND SHRINKING SLICES = THE SLICES ARE BIGGER
Growing pies and shrinking slices - if there’s a singular framework to tackle tech debates, this is it. As we look across the group, there are plenty of case studies and examples of companies that have to fight off existential debates that hinge on competition - Meta and TikTok, Google and ChatGPT, Uber and AVs, Amazon vs. everyone else in eCommerce, and Hyperscale clouds and Neo-clouds/AI clouds. In every instance, the TAM expands - more minutes, more searches, more rides, more compute - and the market leader’s share usually gets smaller. As we look a 2026, four key battlegrounds:
Time spent and the digital ads ceiling - there isn’t one. More players, increasing amounts of time spent online. One would think we should have hit a ceiling a while back, but attention keeps moving online, as does associated commercial activity - we’re still at only \~23% eCom penetration of retail. We expect this to continue in 2026 with OpenAI expected to launch an ad product alongside a possibility re-invigorated domestic TikTok. There will still be enough ad dollars for the best placed incumbents to grow, yet we refer to the point above where the platforms that can deliver material AI-led ad creative, targeting, and conversions are the ones to win - Meta, Google, Amazon.
Search and Google’s dominance. LLMs are changing the search landscape. However, these tools have clearly expanded the pie of queries and haven’t posed a significant threat (yet) to commercial search intent. We expect to see OpenAI lean into an advertising model to improve monetization of free users in 2026. Thus far, Search volumes have accelerated and show little signs of slowing down.
Grocery and Amazon’s last frontier. Amazon is doubling down on low-cost, fast-delivery in grocery, competing more directly with Walmart, Instacart, DoorDash, and Uber. That said, with grocery eCommerce penetration in the low-teens (in a > \$1T end-market), Amazon’s push appears to be TAM expansive so far. If we’re right, Instacart should re-rate as it chips away at terminal value fears — durability of GTV growth matters most. This also underpins our growth outlook on DoorDash.
• Hyperscale cloud and AI workloads. The rise of a new generation of AI Neoclouds, AI startups, etc. has led to an incredibly expensive race for power, datacenters, and GPUs/ASICs. AI labs and startups are chasing compute, AI developers are chasing LLMs, and the Hyperscalers are chasing power/datacenters/chips. GCP uptake has put Google in the winner’s camp for the time being. AWS’ position in the new world is far more debated, yet rewind a couple of years and no one had AWS growth anywhere near 20% in 2025, let alone accelerating in 2026+. We are believers in AWS acceleration as more capacity comes online. The key question is whether AWS’ smaller slice of the larger AI-filled cloud pie is interesting to investors in light of competitive cloud revenue growth rates, and whether steady-state profitability remains comparable to a non-AI world?
THEME #4: BIG TECH ENCROACHES UPON EVERYTHING… WITH MIXED RESULTS
The largest players have a data and distribution advantage, as well as the ability to outspend the rest of the sector on CapEx and product development. We have seen Big Tech (i.e., Amazon, Google, Tesla) leverage this to increasingly encroach upon new TAMs dominated by smaller platforms (e.g., AVs and rideshare, Amazon and grocery, Google’s experimentation around Pinterest and Zillow like offerings).

THEME #1: AI GOES FROM MODEL & PRODUCT COMPARISONS → PRODUCT & REVENUE COMPARISONS
An AI success story starts with a strong model, but that is only the beginning of establishing a strong Consumer Internet business. While the models continue to improve, the rate of change has gotten more marginal and consumer awareness of model capabilities is also debatable. With models becoming more commoditized, we expect the focus to shift from model efficacy to a combination of user KPIs and monetization — i.e., which AI tools offer the best products that attract the most users and user engagement, achieve the fastest growth, and generate real AI revenue.
We identify four trends underpinning this theme: (1) The race for durable engagement; (2) Third-party integrations to spur commercial behavior; (3) companies that can layer into existing experiences and workflows to drive productivity and revenue enhancements; and (4) tapering CapEx intensity from the major Hyperscalers, even as CapEx dollars grow.
EXHIBIT 3: We see four phases of Gen AI success, and believe the focus in 2026 will shift increasingly to an assessment of Product & Revenue when evaluating winners/losers.
RECURRING ENGAGEMENT METRICS TAKE CENTER STAGE
While the underlying models appear to be get commoditized, the race to win the consumer (from a product and adoption standpoint), is consolidating towards a two-horse race between OpenAI and Alphabet. And we expect the narrative in 2026 to be driven by recurring engagement metrics.

THEME #3: GROWING PIES AND SHRINKING SLICES...THE SLICES ARE BIGGER
We repeatedly observe a pattern of disruption risk that sparks concerns around market share erosion for the incumbents. This can be competition from a larger player (e.g., Amazon in grocery) and/or technological advancement from a newer entrant (e.g., OpenAI in Search and Waymo in AVs/rideshare). The market then grapples with the conundrum of “Growing pies and shrinking slices” amongst this new competitive subset.
Rideshare and AVs is one clear example of this dynamic. Others include: (1) Time spent and the digital ads ceiling; (2) Search and Google’s dominance; (3) Amazon in Grocery; and (4) Hyperscale Cloud and Amazon’s position as the market leader.
TIME SPENT ONLINE AND THE DIGITAL ADS CEILING
The digital ads sector has withstood TikTok’s growth into a large-scale, global platform. Initially perceived as a major threat, short-form videos ultimately also benefited Meta. While Meta lost some share of time to TikTok, it embraced the new form factor. From 2021 to 2025, user time on Meta platforms compounded at 8% with Instagram growing a 15% CAGR — only slightly below TikTok’s 16% CAGR.
We do not believe that Digital Ads has hit a ceiling and remain constructive on fundamentals, with revenue for the digital ads basket accelerating to \~18% in 3Q25. AI can benefit both engagement and monetization rates. And if AI unlocks more online commerce (e.g., eCommerce and online travel), that’s also good for digital ads. Meta is our top pick in Digital Ads and checks a lot of these boxes. Tangential to the broader debate on Search dollars, the allocation of retail media dollars will be heavily debated in an AI-first world.
