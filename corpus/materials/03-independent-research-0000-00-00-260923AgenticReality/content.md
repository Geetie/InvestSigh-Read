The Ramifications of Agentic Consumer Adoption
![001_p002_00.jpg](figures/001_p002_00.jpg)

Seven months ago, we pontificated on a scenario called The 2028 Global Intelligence Crisis – a future in which AI 
disruptions sent ripples across the global economy. In this thought experiment, we made no directional market calls but 
rather laid out a series of potential disruptions and how they could interact. Our caveat, that we would be “right about a 
few and almost certainly wrong about more” got overlooked by a crowd more concerned with the next three months of 
price action.
But things are changing – and we are already beginning to see where our thoughts about the impact of agents removing 
friction were right and wrong. People are starting to take notice…perhaps most hilariously summed up by our friend 
BuccoCapital (second time one of his tweets has made it into an article, third time gets a prize).
Obviously, the “Citrini Trade” is based off of our February article. But this has been a focus for us since the beginning, our 
first article was on the beneficiaries of the AI trade, but our second was on the losers - titled “Less Deus, More Machina”. 
And while each individual instance may appear to be “sell first, ask questions later”, the cumulative effect will - in our view - 
be a gradual pricing in of the most likely to occur disruptions due to AI.
After all, we’re spending trillions of dollars, nobody is left that doubts this is a paradigm shift in technology. It’s unlikely that 
this is the only paradigm shift that causes disruptions to incumbent business models.
Just as the market has evolved in its understanding, so have we. And we’ve come a long way from 2023’s hate-selling of 
Chegg… back in February, most of the people selling the intermediaries were using a frame of reference that was “the 
chatbot can show you a hotel”. Now, we’re past the tipping point.
Consumer-facing agents have been kicking around for a while but Meta’s consumer agent – Muse, with a free tier at 100 
million tokens per week – seems like a watershed moment, not necessarily because of its technical abilities but because of 
its reach.
Relative to other bots out there, it feels less robust than other solutions. Yet that’s the lament of every tech geek when a 
more consumer friendly version enters the market. It probably means they are on the right track. Meta is deliberately 
targeting consumers, not enterprises, with Muse.
In our little bubble, “AI Agents” have been a topic of discussion for the past few years. But, we think it’s safe to say that this 
is the first time the broader population will derive utility from consumer agents. Muse is achieving product market fit, 
having rocketed to the #1 spot in the App Store, with more than 700,000 downloads on the first day.
Agentic’s Studio Ghibli moment is here.

First, let’s revisit our thought piece from February:
“By early 2027, LLM usage had become default. People were using AI agents who didn’t even know what an AI agent was, in 
the same way people who never learned what “cloud computing” was used streaming services. They thought of it the same 
way they thought of autocomplete or spell-check - a thing their phone just did now.
Qwen’s open-source agentic shopper was the catalyst for AI handling consumer decisions. Within weeks, every major AI 
assistant had integrated some agentic commerce feature. Distilled models meant these agents could run on phones and 
laptops, not just cloud instances, reducing the marginal cost of inference significantly.
The part that should have unsettled investors more than it did was that these agents didn’t wait to be asked. They ran in the 
background according to the user’s preferences. Commerce stopped being a series of discrete human decisions and became 
a continuous optimization process, running 24/7 on behalf of every connected consumer. By March 2027, the median 
individual in the United States was consuming 400,000 tokens per day - 10x since the end of 2026.
The next link in the chain was already breaking.
Intermediation.
Over the past fifty years, the U.S. economy built a giant rent-extraction layer on top of human limitations: things take time, 
patience runs out, brand familiarity substitutes for diligence, and most people are willing to accept a bad price to avoid 
more clicks. Trillions of dollars of enterprise value depended on those constraints persisting.
It started out simple enough. Agents removed friction.
Subscriptions and memberships that passively renewed despite months of disuse. Introductory pricing that sneakily doubled 
after the trial period. Each one was rebranded as a hostage situation that agents could negotiate. The average customer 
lifetime value, the metric the entire subscription economy was built on, distinctly declined.
Consumer agents began to change how nearly all consumer transactions worked.
Humans don’t really have the time to price-match across five competing platforms before buying a box of protein bars. 
Machines do.
Travel booking platforms were an early casualty, because they were the simplest. By Q4 2026, our agents could assemble a 
complete itinerary (flights, hotels, ground transport, loyalty optimization, budget constraints, refunds) faster and cheaper 
than any platform.
Insurance renewals, where the entire renewal model depended on policyholder inertia, were reformed. Agents that re-shop 
your coverage annually dismantled the 15-20% of premiums that insurers earned from passive renewals.
Financial advice. Tax prep. Routine legal work. Any category where the service provider’s value proposition was ultimately “I 
will navigate complexity that you find tedious” was disrupted, as the agents found nothing tedious.
Even places we thought insulated by the value of human relationships proved fragile. Real estate, where buyers had 
tolerated 5-6% commissions for decades because of information asymmetry between agent and consumer, crumbled once 
AI agents equipped with MLS access and decades of transaction data could replicate the knowledge base instantly. A sellside piece from March 2027 titled it “agent on agent violence”. The median buy-side commission in major metros had 
compressed from 2.5-3% to under 1%, and a growing share of transactions were closing with no human agent on the buy 
side at all.
We had overestimated the value of “human relationships”. Turns out that a lot of what people called relationships was 
simply friction with a friendly face.
That was just the start of the disruption for the intermediation layer. Successful companies had spent billions to effectively 
exploit quirks of consumer behavior and human psychology that didn’t matter anymore.
Machines optimizing for price and fit do not care about your favorite app or the websites you’ve been habitually opening for 
the last four years, nor feel the pull of a well-designed checkout experience. They don’t get tired and accept the easiest 
option or default to “I always just order from here”.”

It’s far from a fait accompli, but if you look at the moves in the market since Muse came into the zeitgeist you’d be hard 
pressed to say we haven’t benefitted from beginning to think about this back in February (actually, back in June 2023) 
instead of last week. We got some things right, and other things wrong (the card companies are probably not going to be 
the losers here). But it does seem like many of the other stars are aligning.
Tactics that worked when consumer behavior was dictated by human psychology will fall by the wayside, and those that 
fail to adapt will be left behind.
Every day from now on we will see new examples of areas where friction was profitable becoming cost centers for 
companies that don’t react in a timely enough manner.
Today you might realize just how many flight credits, loyalty points or promotional offers you’ve never used that will end 
up utilized by agents. You may use an agent to save you money on subscriptions, services or recurring expenses. “What 
impact will that have?” is a question that won’t be asked just by niche investment research firms, but by the populace at 
large.
Tomorrow, you might see something that makes you ask how much money health insurers make simply because people 
won’t sit on the phone for 5 hours trying to get coverage approved unless it’s a massive expense and/or life or death 
matter.
What about your savings account or cash balances earning little to no yield? That money could be in a money market fund, 
but you haven’t bothered to move it. How much in net interest income is made because of that friction?
The coming years will be some of the most interesting for investors who are intrigued by paradigm shifts and their ripple 
effects.
Thankfully, with the benefit of more information and observing real-world implementation, it feels like an apt time to 
revisit the question:
Who wins? And who loses?
Revisiting Our Agentic Frameworks 
We’ve been writing about the impact of AI since we began, and not just limited to the data center.
We can see the back-end, though. Despite our efforts to cover the second and third-order impacts from AI use rather than 
the first-order impact from its scaling, we know many of you have glossed over these in favor of reading yet another article 
about semis supply chain bottlenecks. That’s okay, that was the right thing to do in the first half. But now the market is a 
bit different.
So, rather than repeat in depth, here’s the recap:
[Original Framework] In June 2023, our second ever paid article was titled Less Deus, More Machina and detailed losers 
due to AI in chatbot form (with some hints at the potential for agentic disruption). Our standalone short basket heavily 
favored call centers, BPO, stock images, expense products, staffing agencies, legal tech and no-code website building 
solutions.

SPY climbed 75% since publication while our short basket dropped nearly 40%. However, many of these businesses are 
either extremely small now or consensus shorts at high risk of a squeeze.
The underlying question of the piece, though, remains useful…how much of a company’s economics survives when its 
customers can accomplish the same task more easily? Consumer agents extend that question to the effort involved in 
comparing, switching, negotiating and cancelling.
So far, in 2026, our coverage has been primarily centered on the impact of Agents – the shift from “who builds it” to “who 
uses it”. And for the most part, we’ve been looking for the beneficiaries rather than losers.
[Cybersecurity] Earlier this year the market had a conniption when it understood the second order effects of coding bots. 
The baby, along with the bathwater, was thrown out in a fire sale of software equities. Some of the selloff was justified, but 
we felt there was a large overreaction.
“Selling cybersecurity names because of agentic coding advancements is, for lack of a more apt word, stupid.
If AI agents are truly about to proliferate across every enterprise on Earth, performing tasks autonomously, accessing 
systems, making API calls, and moving data around, does that make cybersecurity less important? No. With great 
confidence, definitely not. And yet the market sold CRWD, NET, RBRK, PANW and ZS alongside TEAM and CRM as if they were 
the same trade.”
[Infrastructure Software, Networking, Observability] In our Agentic Utilities piece from March, we saw that 
infrastructure software businesses – spanning CPaaS, networking, observability, and more – were unfairly punished for the 
risks posed to its GICS peers. Meanwhile, the evolving character of the internet and the explosion of agentic AI meant that 
these services would be even more vital during the forthcoming cycle.
![004_p006_03.jpg](figures/004_p006_03.jpg)

[Strategically-Aligned Retailers] Then, in June’s State of the Themes, we highlighted Shopify (SHOP US) as a winner 
amidst the disaggregation of online shopping. The stock had also been caught in the wash of the software selloff, despite 
the fact that AI would empower the long tail of retailers to meet the bespoke needs of consumers.
“Shopify (SHOP US): A second-order winner of the agentic boom lives in the e-commerce universe – specifically, the long tail 
of retailers. Disaggregation and unique purchasing habits are levered to AI, as consumer tastes are more accurately and 
uniquely targeted – driving increased conversions for niche products”
[Meta] Finally, in All Along the AI Watchtower, we borrowed a term from our high school maths teachers in PEMDAS 
(Please Excuse Meta’s Deliberate AI Spending), where we rebuked the market’s view that it was the black sheep of the 
hyperscaler complex and likened the setup to Google circa July 2025.
“Despite the prevailing negative narrative on Google (GOOGL US) web search and Gemini disappointment, we highlighted the 
company as an unappreciated winner and it’s up nearly 80% since. Now, a year later, does Meta (META US) provide a similar 
setup?”
(It’s certainly starting to look like it).
Our broad lines of thinking haven’t changed, but we continue to sharpen our views as this moves from theory to real world 
observation.
The idea is simple: agents work for consumers. Their raison d’être is to make people’s lives better by getting them the best 
products and the best prices, personalized to their preferences, without the hassle.
The clearest cut beneficiaries are those that either build the agents, make them better, or provide the infrastructure for 
them to operate. The losers are those that have no way to adapt to the new way of doing things.
As we stand today, here are three ways to play the Agentic Reality:
Beneficiaries: Agentic Enablers
Pairs: Disintermediation
Losers: Subscriptions and Aggregators

Beneficiaries:  First and foremost is Meta Platforms (META US).
After months of choppy headlines, Meta’s AI team (headed up by Scale founder Alexandr Wang) finally has a consumer hit. 
A big one: Muse. Ten days after its launch this month, Muse reached No. 1 on Apple’s U.S. free iPhone app chart, with a 4.9 
rating.
Muse is basically a personal assistant for everyone. It can remember your preferences, work across your apps, use a 
browser to carry out tasks and keep going after you close the app — asking for approval before sensitive actions such as 
purchases.
“The muse reception has honestly been beyond our biggest dreams,” Wang said, on his very active X account, where he posted 
dozens of times in recent days highlighting the response to Muse.
With Muse, Meta has gained access to an incredible wealth of data. Your calendar, other apps, shopping habits, email. And 
like we said earlier this summer, Meta has the infrastructure to turn that data into dollars very quickly.
We’re seeing people use Muse not just to buy things, but to cancel things. That is extremely useful behavioral data. Meta 
has positioned themselves to gain insight into consumers in a manner that should take their ARPU much higher. Most of 
that lift will come from ads – think about how effective Meta can become at delivering ads with this incremental insight. 
That’s before you even consider the potential for them to work out some sort of deal where they participate in the 
commerce enabled by their app.
Continuing to think from the first-order, our regular partners in investigation – Hunterbrook – have turned up a beneficiary 
of Meta’s Muse success that is almost certainly being overlooked. Who is upstream of Meta’s success here?
Upstream Highlight: Innodata (INOD US)  
Meta is Innodata’s largest customer, according to interviews with former employees and reviews of job postings. 
In 2024, Innodata had 400 full-time equivalent employees working on the Meta account, according to a former 
director. In the years since, that business has expanded.
In August, Innodata disclosed a program focused on “personalization of long-horizon agents” with its largest 
customer. The technical description of the project points to Muse, though neither company has confirmed the 
product connection. Neither Meta nor Innodata denied the relationship despite requests for comment.
Innodata’s opportunity extends beyond one app. Like Mercor and Scale AI, its offerings now span advanced 
training data, computer-use environments, and reusable datasets — not just conventional data labeling. And earlier 
this year, Innodata disclosed winning a new frontier AI lab as a customer, though more than two-thirds of its 2026 
revenue so far has been attributable to its two largest customers.
Founded in 1988, Innodata spent decades doing the unglamorous work of turning documents into digitized, structured 
information. Today, alongside that older business, it supplies the training material, human judgment, and testing that help 
AI models improve. It doesn’t have to build the next great model to make money. It can get paid to help teach it.
That prospect helped turn a roughly $4 stock in early 2023 into a $121.50 stock at its June closing high. At the center of the 
transformation was a customer Innodata did not name in its financial statements. By 2025, that customer accounted for 
approximately $146 million — 58% of the company’s revenue. The mystery customer was Meta.
That dependence cuts both ways. Meta’s investment in Scale raised an uncomfortable question: Why keep paying Innodata 
if you’ve just bought almost half of a larger competitor? The latest results added to that concern: The largest account’s 
quarterly revenue fell by roughly a third. Innodata’s shares are now back near $57. The customer behind its breakout has 
also become its most consequential risk.

Yet interviews with former employees, public hiring records, and the company’s own disclosures point less to a customerclient relationship and more to a deeply embedded partnership. Innodata personnel have worked inside Meta’s AI 
organization across multiple projects. And Innodata’s new assignment from its largest customer — personalizing agents 
that perform long, complicated tasks — closely resembles the work behind Muse.
In an interview with Hunterbrook Media, one former employee, whose work covered new sales and service lines, pushed 
back on the fear that Scale would displace Innodata. Asked whether Meta had started cutting its outside help after the 
Scale investment, they said: “If anything, I would say it’s more.”
This is certainly the narrative Innodata has been propagating.
“With our largest customer, we continue to grow as we diversify into more organizations and more AI workflows and partner 
with them on their flagship next-generation AI program,” Innodata shared in its most recent earnings call, in which the 
company also announced its President would be taking over as CEO.”
Teaching models to carry out complicated tasks can require fresh material: expert-written answers, worked examples and 
simulated situations in which to practice. That demand helped take Mercor from a $250 million valuation in 2024 to $10 
billion in 2025. Meta made an even bigger bet: $14.3 billion for a 49% stake in Scale AI.
The strongest clue to a Muse assignment comes from Innodata itself. In August, it announced “a significant new program 
with our largest customer covering personalization of long-horizon agents - now scaling.”
Meta’s description of Muse Spark 1.3, which powers the assistant, sounds strikingly similar: “When working on long tasks, it 
adapts to user preferences.” The timing fits, too. Meta says Muse had been developed and used internally since early 2026. 
Innodata’s program was scaling ahead of September’s launch. The customer, task, and timing point toward Muse — or at 
least the shared models behind it. Neither company has publicly identified the assignment as Muse-related.
And Meta is no longer carrying the story alone. A second, unnamed Big Tech customer more than doubled its quarterly 
contribution to roughly $31 million, almost matching the largest account. Innodata also disclosed winning a new frontier AI 
lab.

Now, Innodata is expanding to datasets designed for agentic use-cases as the company said in its August 6 press-release.
“Through our research efforts, we have established an early position in agentic reinforcement learning, one of the most 
important frontiers in AI development, and a second program covering reinforcement-learning environments for computeruse agentic tasks.”
What, exactly, does this mean?
Innodata is now building training material for AI that acts, instead of just answering questions. The company says its 
simulated environments teach agents to “reason, recover from errors, and complete complex, long-horizon tasks.” This 
training material comes populated with fictional people, emails, documents and databases. The data is deliberately 
complicated and incomplete – you can think of it as a practice version of the messy digital life that an AI assistant will be 
eventually asked to manage.
Consider an instruction as simple as “plan my trip to Paris.” An assistant like Muse might need to check your calendar, 
compare flights, find a hotel, arrange transportation, and ask for approval before spending your money.
Along the way, it might notice that an old email lists dates you have since changed. It also might recall your preference for 
nonstop flights. It’ll also help distinguish the restaurant that your friend had recommended instead of the one they told 
you to avoid.
These are the kinds of challenges behind Meta’s promise of an assistant that works across applications and remembers 
what matters to its user.
Some parts have checkable answers: Did the agent select the right dates? Stay within budget? Obtain permission? Innodata 
says it builds automated checks for those kinds of outcomes.
But other decisions depend on judgment. Is saving $100 worth a six-hour layover? Does “somewhere lively” mean a 
neighborhood full of restaurants — or a room above a nightclub? A reservation can be perfectly valid and still be a terrible 
choice. The challenge is not merely teaching the assistant to book a trip. It is teaching it to understand what a good trip 
means to the person asking.
That is where human judgment becomes training data. Innodata says its reviewers score AI responses and explain their 
mistakes, following guidelines intended to “objectify subjectivity.”
For a company like Meta, the potential value is an assistant that learns when to act, when to ask, and when mission 
accomplished is not quite the same as a job well done.

We’ve gotten a few things right in the above, as far as market price action is concerned, and a few things incorrect. But we

![014_p016_13.jpg](figures/014_p016_13.jpg)

Subscription companies employ four methods to contend against churn.
Tedium rent is predicated on the fact that people are impatient. Most of us would rather eat the cost of an unused 
subscription than have to pick up the phone, wait on hold for several minutes, and then listen to a customer service rep try 
to dissuade us from cancelling.
Fortunately for the consumer, agents now have telephony capabilities. They don’t feel the effects of linear time and are 
willing to wait on hold indefinitely, even withstanding that horrible elevator music. As far as we know, sales scripts aren’t 
going to persuade an AI, so those barriers to exit are now demolished.
Inertia rent works because people are lazy. We are content with letting unused subscriptions renew because the 
alternative would mean having to jump through the requisite hoops to cancel or shop for a new rate. The agent’s natural 
state, by contrast, is to be hyperaware – constantly evaluating your expense footprint to find redundancies and 
inefficiencies.
Attention rent is a blend of inertia and tedium. We are inundated with ads for things that we don’t think about 99% of the 
time. But, we have finite attention spans and are prone to recency bias. We make purchases based on convenience – which 
speaks to the potency of big brand advertising.
Machines have no habits, and aren’t influenced by ads. They will shop for the best deals, and can objectively evaluate 
several alternatives simultaneously.
Finally, asymmetry rent is synonymous with aggregation. The platform knows more than the customer does, meaning 
they are entitled to a cut of the transaction for the effort of orchestration. A Finder’s Fee, if you will.
Of course, when information is commodified, that asymmetry withers, and aggregation is less important. An agent with 
access to that information can upend incumbent aggregator models in just a few prompts.
We anticipate that agents will begin to cut in on margins for companies that have built up their moat around rentseeking on top of tedium, inertia, attention and/or asymmetry.

Think about auto insurance for a moment. When you use Muse or Instinct to get quotes from the insurers, they are going 
directly to the insurance companies themselves – effectively removing the middle man of “browsing the web.”
What does that mean for the web properties that help you compare insurance? They’re deprecated, left to wither on the 
vine of the Old Internet.
Put another way, Agents are attempting to replace the application layer of the internet, which puts the incumbents at risk.
This is still early, and we don’t know how Meta (and others) want to play this out. Muse confirmed that Booking.com (BKNG 
US) and other intermediaries can be used for hotel searches as there’s enough of a price difference to look there as well as 
the hotel brand’s website.
There’s also the monetization side: we’ve yet to see a consumer AI product that has seen any kind of success with ads, and 
if anyone’s going to crack that code, it’s Zuckerberg. Instead of elbowing them out, Meta may choose to work with the 
intermediaries – Booking spends $8 billion on marketing, some of that on Meta’s ad platform. The math might work that 
killing a flock of golden geese is bad for the egg-laying business.
Companies that are screwed here are the ones that help consumers make better personal finance (or shopping) 
decisions.

One of the longest standing and irrational freebies are retail brokerages charging far off-market rates on cash balance and 
loans. These excessive (some might say obscene) rents charged by the big legacy platforms (Schwab, E-Trade, Fidelity, 
Vanguard) only exist because of consumer ignorance and the friction of switching platforms.

Money Markets  
Traditional wealth advisors have long extracted fees by providing personal reassurance to an area daunting to many: their 
finances. But now, the idea of calling up your financial advisor, and having them pitch a complex structured product or 
high-fee mutual fund seems antiquated. The first line of “expert opinion” is increasingly going to LLMs and agents.

Even a modest nest egg earns enough to move the needle at 4% risk free rates, and the ability to quickly plunk cash into a 
money market fund has never been easier, with much lower overhead than many traditional financial products. This is 
some of the lowest hanging fruit an agent with access to your financials might suggest. We’ve already seen this headline

On the long side, we choose Federated Hermes (FHI US) which specializes in money markets. On the short side, 
traditional wealth advisory firm LPL Financial Holdings (LPLA US).

Property insurance falls in the same category. Customers still need insurance, and we’re all a little exhausted from having 
to shop around for the best rate. But, the annual renewal becomes a lot less arduous if the agent is automatically reshopping the policy every year.

This too, warrants a degree of nuance. Agencies operate on the asymmetry rent – while the direct carriers are likely to

Travel  
How about travel? We asked Muse whether she used airline websites or third party:
Neither, really. When I search flights, I use a flight booking service called Duffel, which is a professional booking system that 
pulls inventory from the airlines directly. So it’s not me clicking around airline sites, and it’s not a consumer third-party site

Duffel bypasses traditional consumer aggregators like Expedia (EXPE US), Booking (BKNG US), and TripAdvisor (TRIP

ultimate service provider – the hotel, airline, or rental car – still gets the customer.
On the long leg, we choose a quadfecta of hotels that can benefit from “direct” bookings via agents – Marriott (MAR US),

Financial Data  
While an agent can explain your credit report, find a better loan and help you apply, the lender still needs to know whether 
you’ll repay it. That’s not going to be disintermediated by AI anytime soon, in our view.
But it is eminently possible that the price of financial guidance can fall while demand for the information increases.
Equifax (EFX US) and Experian (EXPN LN) sit underneath many of the services consumers interact with. Their databases, 
data-provider relationships and verification capabilities supply information that a more capable model cannot simply 
generate. As agents make it easier to compare financial products and complete applications, there is an opportunity for 
additional paid credit checks, income verifications and fraud screening. The consumer may change how they shop while 
the lender continues buying the same underlying information.
Credit Karma provides Equifax and TransUnion reports for free, then typically earns a commission when a customer is 
approved for a recommended product. Gen Digital also uses Equifax data across its identity-protection and financial 
offerings. We can therefore own a supplier while shorting businesses whose economics depend more heavily on 
controlling the consumer relationship.
For Gen, the pressure comes from a customer who now has help scrutinizing every renewal. Norton and LifeLock have 
useful features, but an agent can identify overlapping coverage, compare alternatives and help the customer switch. The 
services can remain useful while the price consumers are willing to pay for the package declines.
Intuit faces a different version of the same problem. Credit Karma’s free service monetizes the journey from financial 
information to a product recommendation. If an agent takes over that journey, referral economics become more 
contested. An application routed away from Credit Karma can still produce revenue for a bureau. TurboTax adds exposure 
to the declining cost of financial administration: gathering documents and navigating a cheaper preparation service 
become less daunting when the customer has assistance.
Experian also has an opportunity to verify the agents themselves. Its announced Agent Trust framework links verified 
consumers to agents acting on their behalf, with collaborators including Visa, Cloudflare and Skyfire. That remains a 
developing commercial opportunity, but it gives us a concrete example of agent adoption creating another use for identity 
information.

There are crosscurrents. Experian has consumer subscriptions and marketplaces of its own. QuickBooks’ accounting 
records and embedded workflows give Intuit a more durable business, and Intuit is already distributing its products 
through Claude. Our bet is that verified data retains more pricing power as the consumer interface changes.
Retail  
The disaggregation mechanism will also unfold across the retail ecosystem. Companies like Uber (UBER US) and Instacart 
(CART US) are textbook aggregators – packaging a collection of non-exclusive suppliers (drivers, grocers). Their business 
model banks on the fact that people are either too impatient or too lazy to go through the process of evaluating their 
options, making a decision, calling the restaurant or visiting the store, and making the transaction.
As we said back in February:
“Agents accelerated both sides of the destruction. They enabled the competitors and then they used them. The DoorDash 
moat was literally ‘you’re hungry, you’re lazy, this is the app on your home screen.’ An agent doesn’t have a home screen. It 
checks DoorDash, Uber Eats, the restaurant’s own site, and twenty new vibe-coded alternatives so it can pick the lowest fee 
and fastest delivery every time.”
Now, an agent-native food delivery platform has yet to emerge from this megatrend, but the disintermediation risk is 
legitimate. AI agents are far more likely to go directly to the restaurant itself, rather than bank on the convenience of 
DoorDash’s sleek UI.
We are simply highlighting this reasoning again - UBER, CART volumes probably go unchanged as people go from using the 
apps to using agents…but that could impact advertising revenue in the short term. And in the long term, we do believe that 
we will see an agentic ecosystem where every player in the chain has an agent and there is enough incentive for new 
entrants to spin up competitors like Empower.
Under this pairing, we’d short the intermediaries (UBER, CART) against a long expression of the origin. Those are grocery 
stores and chains like Walmart (WMT US) and Costco (COST US).
Cellular

Cell phone carriers have much more wiggle room to negotiate, and while there’s overlap with internet and phone (see 
AT&T), the risk is with the former business. Agents can negotiate with companies not just on price, but also contract 
extensions with new phones as the incentive program.
T-Mobile (TMUS US) stands out as a relative winner under this regime as fixed wireless expense is the relief valve for 
customers looking for savings on wireless connectivity.
Charter Communications (CHTR US) and Comcast (CMCSA US) stand out as losers on the wrong side of agentic

Losers: Subscriptions & Aggregators 
In the Global Intelligence Crisis, we posited that agentic assistants posed a threat to the subscription revenue model. The 
idea being that many of these subscriptions renew despite not being used. An agent can seamlessly monitor these 
subscriptions and cancel them when they see they’re not being utilized.
![021_p024_20.jpg](figures/021_p024_20.jpg)
![022_p024_21.jpg](figures/022_p024_21.jpg)

The consumer-facing subscription business rests on a fairly straightforward assumption: the customer has a “lifetime 
value” (LTV) – a calculated guess on how much that customer relationship is worth over time.
That LTV estimate sets the ceiling on what a company can afford to spend on acquiring the customer. In industry terms: a 
customer acquisition cost, or CAC. The objective of consumer subscription businesses is to ensure that the LTV is an 
order of magnitude greater than the CAC.

The Agentic Reality asks the question: what happens when you remove friction from the subscription model? What if 
the lazy customer – through no effort of their own – suddenly became hyper-vigilant, and not only remembers to cancel 
their subscription, but is now looking to bargain for a better rate?
The use case for cutting subscriptions is salient enough to lure OpenAI back into the consumer market.
Inbound Email from OpenAI
Both sides of the LTV/CAC equation are now under attack. The former – LTV – is weighed down by consumer agents 
truncating the duration of the customer relationship. The latter – CAC – suffers as retention becomes more expensive.

The consumer-facing companies at highest risk of an agentic rugpull are those that had high switching costs until 
yesterday. Some of those costs are because there are only a few alternatives. Others were effective at weaponizing human 
nature and biases to ensure that you never churned.
Critically, these techniques don’t work against AI agents.

Aggregators  
Aggregators have also operated under a static assumption: information asymmetry between buyers and sellers warrants a 
“take rate” from whoever runs the platform. It’s a fair proposition, but frankly, the fees have gotten out of hand.
![036_p030_35.jpg](figures/036_p030_35.jpg)
![037_p030_36.jpg](figures/037_p030_36.jpg)
![038_p030_37.jpg](figures/038_p030_37.jpg)

NerdWallet (NRDS US) is the textbook definition of this model. The company hosts a variety of financial services products 
on their platform – ranging from consumer lending, auto insurance, savings accounts, and mortgages. Crucially, they are 
not the ones underwriting or offering any of these products. That task is instead undertaken by the companies who 
advertise on their platform. In exchange, NerdWallet generates sales on ad interactions, clicks, and impressions.
NerdWallet is joined by LendingTree (TREE US), EverQuote (EVER US), MediaAlpha (MAX US), and QuinStreet (QNST 
US) in employing this model. Performance marketing extracts the asymmetry rent – or the fact that consumers simply 
aren’t always equipped with the best information when making a decision. The proliferation of agentic distribution makes 
that risk a little more tangible. As agents eschew the marketplace model in favor of going direct to the source, that works 
against the performance marketing model upon which the internet’s earliest winners were built on.
As Agentic traffic replaces human, these networks start to decay. Information gets old, accounts get stale, and the 
company’s value as an intermediary dissipates.
